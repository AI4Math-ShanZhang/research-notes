MOSS-Video-Preview — Real-Time Video Understanding via Cross-Attention

Paper: Pengyu Wang, Chenkun Tan, Shaojun Zhou, Wei Huang, Qirui Zhou et al., corresponding Xipeng Qiu (Fudan University · Shanghai Innovation Institute) · arXiv:2606.07639 · code. Reading log — the paper rewritten in plain language, with the KV-cache difference worked out step by step.

Base: Llama-3.2-11B-Vision video → text only no video generation 4 training stages 836K real-time samples
Why this sits in the Unified Multimodal category. It is not a unified model — frames in, text out, nothing flows back to the video. It is filed here because its central trick is the cleanest statement of a constraint any unified model runs into: the cache-side role (encode once, park it, stay out of the sequence) and the sequence-side role (decode step by step) are mutually exclusive, and MOSS is fast precisely because vision is permanently assigned to the condition role. A unified model cannot make that assignment, so it cannot reuse this fix.

1. The one problem the paper attacks

The whole paper hangs on a single sentence, which they repeat several times:

Perception must not be blocked by generation.

Read that as: while the model is writing an answer, it should still be watching.

Three ways to watch a video

ModeCan it answer before the video ends?Does it keep watching while answering?
OfflineNo — waits for the whole clip—
Streaming (what exists today)YesNo
Real-time (this paper)YesYes

The paper is careful about one thing, and it is worth repeating because it is easy to get wrong:

The problem is not that a streaming model can never fix a wrong answer. It can — on the next turn. The problem is that the fix is not timely. It always arrives one reply too late.

Their words: “The distinction is not whether an answer can be revised, but whether the revision is timely.”

Why one reply late is a real failure

Watching someone cook. Suppose a reply takes 2 seconds to write.

real world 0s -------- 1s -------- 2s -------- 3s -------- 4s frames 1..10 11..20 21..30 31..40 model [reads 1-10] [ writing about frames 1-10 ............ ] ^ answer comes out at 3s but describes second 1 t=1.4s the pan falls <-- model does not see this t=2.0s a fire starts <-- model does not see this t=3.0s model finishes the sentence about oil, then finally looks

The answer is not wrong. It matches the frames it read. It is simply late, and for anything live — driving, a robot arm, a blind-assistance device — being correct about the past is the same as being wrong.

2. What the streaming baseline actually does

Almost every video LLM today is decoder-only. There is one token sequence. Frames become tokens in it. Text becomes tokens in it. Same sequence, same self-attention.

So taking in a frame means writing into the exact sequence the model is currently decoding. One resource, two writers. Existing systems dodge the conflict by taking turns:

The consequence, stated exactly

Let a reply take L seconds.

  • A blind window of length L. Frames arriving in it are either dropped (gone forever) or queued (so the lag grows every turn). You lose information or you lose time.
  • The reply is committed. The model cannot stop mid-sentence, because stopping needs evidence it is not receiving.
  • Therefore the shortest possible correction time is L. A longer, more detailed answer makes the model more blind, not less.
Everything in the rest of this paper exists to bring that correction time down from one reply to one token.

3. Architecture: what changed

They start from Llama-3.2-11B-Vision. That model already has cross-attention — it is in the Flamingo family. So the backbone shape is not their invention. What they changed is listed in their own section 3:

  1. Add temporal position encoding to cross-attention (this is the real modelling change).
  2. Add 2D pooling before injection, to shrink the visual K/V.
  3. Wire up an incremental frame injection path and a streaming interface, so one set of weights serves both offline and real-time.

How vision gets injected

At each of the 8 cross-attention layers:

h  ←  h  +  tanh(g_attn) · CrossAttn(h, V)
h  ←  h  +  tanh(g_ffn)  · FFN(h)

g_attn and g_ffn are single learned numbers. Because of tanh, they start near zero, so at the beginning vision does nothing at all and the block is an identity map. Training slowly opens the gate. This is why adding vision does not smash the language ability of the base model.

The temporal fix — their main modelling change

The bug they found in stock Llama-3.2-Vision: its cross-attention applies no positional encoding at all — not on the queries, not on the keys. So the frames are mutually orderless. The model literally cannot tell which frame came first. For video that is fatal.

Their fix: put RoPE on both sides of cross-attention.

  • A text query uses its normal text position.
  • A visual key uses the position of its <|video|> placeholder inside the interleaved sequence.

Important detail, easy to miss: text and frames share one single position axis. They number the whole interleaved sequence in one pass.

But all visual tokens of one frame share the same index. One frame = one position. So spatial position inside a frame disappears from cross-attention (the ViT already handles that), and what is left is a pure temporal signal: “which frame is this”.

This is why the model needs a fixed resolution: every frame must produce the same number of tokens, so that “one frame, one position” stays regular across the whole sequence.

There is also a cross_attention_mask that says which text span may look at which frames. That is what lets one model handle mixed images, video clips, and multi-turn chat in any order.

4. The KV cache difference — the core of the paper

This is the part worth slowing down on, because on the surface the two designs look identical. In both cases a new frame means: run the encoder, get K/V, append to a cache, next step reads it. The paper openly admits decoder-only can be made real-time this way. So the difference is not capability. It is which cache, and that has consequences.

What happens when a frame arrives mid-sentence

Say the model has already written The person is heating oil and frame 11 arrives right now.

sequenceDiagram participant W as world participant E as ViT + projector participant K as vision K/V cache participant D as decoder step W->>E: frame 11 arrives E->>K: append K/V (8 layers)

Three things make this free:

  1. The vision cache is read-only from the text side. The text never writes into it. Each decoding step issues a fresh query against whatever is currently there. Growing a read-only table between two reads costs nothing — the next read just covers more rows.
  2. Vision K/V does not depend on the text. It comes from the ViT alone. So a cached frame never goes stale when the text context changes. (Compare: the text KV cache does go stale when the prompt changes — that is why other systems need “KV recache”.)
  3. Old frames are reused, never recomputed. Algorithm 1 says this explicitly at two places.

With 256 frames this is not a small effect. It is the whole reason their 11B model beats a 7B decoder-only model on speed: TTFT 1.95 s vs 9.94 s, and 38.4 vs 14.3 tokens per second.

Difference 2 — a frame costs one position, not hundreds

Both designs put text and frames on one position axis. The difference is how much of the axis a frame eats.

baselinea frame = N positions

pos 100..105 "The person is heating oil" pos 106..205 frame 11 tokens ← inserted pos 206 next word of the SAME sentence

oil is at 105 and the next word of the same sentence is at 206. RoPE now sees them 101 apart. In training, consecutive words are 1 apart. Every frame you insert stretches your own sentence.

MOSSa frame = 1 position

pos 100..105 "The person is heating oil" pos 106 frame 11 ← one placeholder pos 107 next word of the SAME sentence

All ~400 visual tokens of frame 11 share index 106. The sentence is stretched by 1, not 101. And the visual tokens themselves are in a different cache, so they never sit between the text tokens in self-attention.

Difference 3 — the channels can be compressed separately

Because vision K/V is its own channel, you can shrink it without touching the text. Their 2D pooling is the first instance: dropping the CLS token and mean-pooling the patch grid cuts tokens per frame to about 1/stride². In decoder-only both token types are in one sequence, so this kind of split is awkward. The paper lists finer channel-wise compression as future work, not as a result.

The honest bottom line. The difference is quantitative, not qualitative. Decoder-only can insert frames mid-reply and the next token will see them. It just costs about 5× the first-token latency, 2.7× slower decoding, and it stretches the model's own sentences by ~100 positions per frame. Cross-attention makes non-blocking the default instead of something you engineer around. That is the paper's actual claim — nothing stronger.

And the cache alone does NOT give you revision

This point is easy to lose. The cache only fixes seeing. A model that sees frame 11 will still happily finish its sentence about frame 10, because language models are strongly biased to complete sentences.

Split the delay in two:

time-to-correction = (time until the model CAN SEE the new frame)     ← the cache
                   + (time until the model DECIDES to change course)  ← tokens + data
baseline streamingMOSS
Term 1 — seeone reply (frames queued, not ingested)one decoding step
Term 2 — decideone reply (re-decides only at the boundary)one decoding step

Fixing only one term gains nothing. Fix only the cache and the model sees frame 11 and keeps talking about frame 10. Fix only the policy and the model is willing to stop but has no new evidence to stop for. Sections 5 and 6 are how they fix term 2.

5. Two new tokens

The vocabulary gains two things that ordinary video LLMs do not have.

<|silence|> — “I am watching but I have nothing to say”

Without it, a model that perceives continuously will describe the same static scene over and over. The paper says this directly.

How it is controlled is unusually simple — it is an inference-time threshold, not a training loss:

at every decoding step:
    p = softmax(logits)
    if p[<|silence|>] >= 0.6:      # τ, the value in the released code
        allowed to stay silent
    else:
        zero out the silence mass, renormalise
        → the model is FORCED to speak

They state explicitly that no extra training-time weighting on <|silence|> is needed — with good enough data, this one threshold is enough. Setting τ at 0.6 biases the model toward answering rather than sitting quiet.

The turning-point token — “stop, something changed”

Decoded as an ellipsis “…”. The model emits it to cut its own sentence short and pivot to new evidence. This is the move a streaming model structurally cannot make.

Because these two tokens are ordinary vocabulary items, the choice continue / pivot / go quiet is re-made at every single decoding step — roughly 38 times per second. Not once per reply.

6. Building the training data

Architecture gives the ability. Data gives the habit. Ordinary video QA cannot teach this habit at all, for two reasons the paper names:

  • Existing data is “whole clip → one answer”. There is no trajectory where the answer gets rewritten.
  • Existing data has no <|silence|> supervision anywhere.
The shape they need: the instruction is fixed, but the best answer evolves as the stream advances.

Their example, which is the clearest thing in the paper:

“What is the score now?”

The question never changes. But when a goal is scored the correct reply must be rewritten. Between goals, the model should stay quiet. Static QA can neither train nor evaluate this.

Starting material: hierarchical captions, not raw video

They first caption every video at three granularities, all timestamped:

  • video level — summarises the whole clip
  • event level — describes event segments
  • action level — details individual actions

For synthesis they flatten to action level, giving an ordered list of (time interval, description) pairs. All the reasoning below happens on text, not pixels — which is why an LLM can drive it.

Phase A — deciding what to say and when

Step 1 · Change-point detection

An LLM reads the caption sequence as if watching it live. For each description it compares against all earlier ones and decides whether the new information is enough to change the current understanding. Three categories:

  • turning point — the state reverses
  • disambiguation / completion — something unclear becomes clear
  • external context change — the surroundings change

The first description has no prior context, so it is never counted as a change.

Step 2 · State-dependent instruction generation

At each change point, treat everything before as known and the new part as Δ. Then write a user question constrained so that:

  • it has a clearly correct answer before Δ,
  • and that answer becomes wrong or incomplete right after Δ,
  • and it targets things that genuinely change over time — actions, relative positions, interactions — not static facts like colour, material, or identity.

For variety the surface form is sampled along three axes (syntactic structure, expressive style, emotional tone) from preset distributions, then all generated questions are pooled and deduplicated by cosine similarity in an embedding space.

Step 3 · Decision–generation response

Given a question Q, first write an anchor answer a₁ from the information up to the change point. Then walk forward segment by segment and make a binary decision each time:

for each following segment:
    does this segment change the best answer to Q?
        yes  → write a new answer a_{k+1} from everything so far
        no   → emit Silence
Two design details that matter more than they look:
  • Silent segments are not written into the response history. Otherwise the model's own past silence would push it toward more silence, and it would drift into never speaking.
  • An already-answered task is explicitly marked, which raises the bar for speaking again. This is what stops it from repeating itself.

Output of Phase A: a pair (Q; a₁, a₂, …) where each aₖ is either real text or Silence.

Phase B — putting it on a clock

Phase A's output is still segment-level. At inference the model reads frames at 1 fps and emits tokens one by one, so supervision has to say what to emit right after each frame. Phase B commits everything onto a per-second timeline.

WhatRuleWhy
Question arrival tq uniform in the first 80% of a₁'s triggering segment guarantees Q is asked before the change, so the first answer is definitely overturned later
Reply start pk uniform in the last third of its triggering segment, with p₁ ≥ tq the model does not reply at the segment boundary — it watches and confirms first. This bakes realistic perception delay into the data.
Reply end qk set by the next utterance so replies meet head-to-tail; if followed by silence, the reply may run into the first half of that silent segment; the last reply runs to video end no gaps or overlaps. Conflicting neighbours are forcibly staggered by at least 1 second.
Text fill 20–60 tokens per second, sampled uniformly, rounded at subword boundaries the random pace is deliberate — it makes the model robust to variable real decoding speed instead of memorising one speed

The two boundary cases (this is where the interrupt behaviour is born)

Text finishes early

Remaining seconds are filled with <|silence|>, plus one extra <|silence|> appended to mark the end of the utterance.

Text overruns

The reply is truncated, the overflow is recorded separately, and a turning-point token is inserted at the start of the next segment.

This is the training signal for “my reply is not finished but the world already moved on.” Without these examples the model would never learn that stopping mid-sentence is a legal move.

Assembly, second by second

Walk from tq to the end of the video. At every second: place a <|video|> placeholder, then either the active reply's text fragment for that second, or <|silence|> if this second is not covered by any reply.

<|video|> text/<|silence|> <|video|> text/<|silence|> <|video|> ...

The finished sample

Their own worked example — read it left to right as time passing:

system: "keep perceiving; adjust when relevant changes occur; output <|silence|> when there is nothing to say" assistant (nobody has asked yet, but it is already watching): <|silence|><|video|><|silence|> user: "What is the score now?" assistant (streaming reply, one second per <|video|>): <|video|> "It's 1-0." <|video|> <|silence|> <|video|> "Now 1-1." <- revised, same question <|video|> <|silence|>

Three things are encoded in that one sample: always-on perception (the silence prelude before any question), revision (1-0 → 1-1 without being asked again), and timely silence (quiet between goals).

Multi-turn assembly

  • Several QAs on the same video are concatenated into a multi-turn dialogue.
  • Every dialogue starts with a pure-silence prelude from video start to tq.
  • Two-turn samples carry one QA.
  • Three-turn samples concatenate two adjacent QAs and, at the moment the second instruction arrives, truncate the first reply at a random ratio — directly teaching “previous answer unfinished, new instruction arrived”.
  • Each sample carries a video field strictly aligned to the frame count, with a timestamp for every <|video|>. Over-long samples are trimmed from the tail; still-too-long ones are skipped.

7. The four training stages

All four use plain next-token prediction, cosine LR with 0.1 warm-up, DeepSpeed ZeRO-2, one epoch each. Each continues from the previous checkpoint.

StageGoalDataScaleTrainable
1Inject vision, align the two modalities image–text pairs15 M ViT, projector, cross-attention

1.5Acquire video temporality 470 K images + 1369 K videos1.8 Mfull model

Real-Time SFTLook-and-revise + silence decision real-time synthetic + offline QA mix836 Kfull model

Why freeze the LLM in stage 1

Only the bridge needs learning — encoder, projector, cross-attention. At that point the tanh gates are near zero and the bridge is unaligned, so unfreezing the text backbone simultaneously would let badly-aligned visual gradients perturb or degrade the language ability. Everything unfreezes from stage 1.5 onward so the whole model can co-adapt.

Why real-time SFT goes last

836 K is small and narrow next to 8 M. Training on it from scratch would damage general understanding. So it continues from the offline checkpoint with the learning rate lowered from 1e-5 to 5e-6 and gradient accumulation 4 for stabler updates. The paper frames it as specialisation, not a fresh run. Mixing offline QA back in prevents the model from collapsing into “always observing, mostly silent” as its only mode.

Two modes, one weight set

The switch between real-time and offline behaviour is entirely in the system prompt. The real-time prompt asks for continuous perception, answers grounded only in what is seen (“Do not make guesses without evidence from visual input”), dynamic adjustment when something relevant changes, and <|silence|> when unable to answer or already finished.

8. Inference, step by step

One model exposes two entry points, differing only in whether frames arrive all at once or over time. Same weights, same cache machinery.

  • offline_generate — whole video and question given upfront
  • real_time_generate — frames arrive over time, tokens stream out, new questions can be interjected mid-generation

The two-state loop

The loop is event-driven. An event is a new frame arrived or a new question arrived — either one alone is enough, and both can arrive together.

stateDiagram-v2 [*] --> Waiting Waiting --> Waiting: event arrives, inject, predict
p(silence) at least 0.6
keep observing, no decoding Waiting --> Replying: event arrives, inject, predict
p(silence) below 0.6
something to say Replying --> Replying: emit next token
continue the reply,
or pivot with the turning-point token
events keep being injected Replying --> Waiting: emitted token is
the silence token

Algorithm 1, in plain words

τ = 0.6
state = Waiting

loop forever:

  if state == Waiting:
      # block until something happens
      if a new frame arrived:
          encode it, append its K/V to the per-layer cache
          (previous frames' K/V is REUSED, not recomputed)
      if a new question arrived:
          append it to the text sequence
      # both may arrive together — inject both

      forward once to get the next-token distribution p
      if p[<|silence|>] >= τ:
          stay in Waiting          # nothing to say, keep watching
      else:
          state = Replying

  else:   # Replying
      if a new frame arrived:
          encode it, append its K/V to the cache
      if a new question arrived:
          insert it into the text sequence

      emit the next token. it may:
          - continue the current reply
          - switch via the turning-point token "…"
          - end the reply

      if the emitted token is <|silence|>:
          state = Waiting
Note where the frame injection sits: inside both branches. That is the entire “perception is not blocked by generation” claim, in code. Nothing is interrupted to ingest a frame — it is just an append before the next token.

The three options mid-reply

step N ... "The person is heating" ↓ frame 11 lands in the cache step N+1 cross-attention now covers frames 1..11 the model picks one of: (a) "oil" → continue, nothing important changed (b) "…" → turning point: cut the sentence, pivot (c) <|silence|> → stop entirely, go back to Waiting

So the granularity of correction is one token, roughly 26 ms at 38 tokens/sec — not one reply.

Does real-time specialisation cost anything?

This was one of their three research questions, and the answer is essentially no. Real-time SFT tracks the offline SFT checkpoint closely, and on several video benchmarks the real-time version is actually higher: VideoMME 62.48 vs 59.81, EgoSchema 54.80 vs 47.40, VSI-Bench 36.20 vs 33.48.

Two outliers diverge without a controlled explanation: SEEDBench is ~19 points higher on real-time, V* is ~13 points higher on offline.

10. What the paper does not show

There is no quantitative real-time evaluation anywhere in the paper. Real-time capability is demonstrated only through three qualitative demos. The authors state this as limitation (1) themselves.

Their reasoning for why is the most interesting paragraph in the paper. To measure real-time you must measure accuracy and timeliness together, because accuracy alone can be gamed by stalling:

A model that waits until well after an event has ended — when the information is unambiguous — scores high on accuracy while completely defeating the purpose of being real-time.

They propose splitting timeliness into two orthogonal parts:

  • system-level — TTFT, max FPS (they do measure this)
  • decision-level — the wait between an event ending and the model choosing to reply (they do not measure this, and no benchmark exists)

Other stated limitations

  • A real gap to SOTA on general benchmarks.
  • Data engineering is preliminary — 836 K synthetic samples, pipeline unrefined and not open-sourced.
  • Ablations are limited. τ = 0.6, the pooling stride, the placement of the 8 cross-attention layers, and the real-time/offline mixing ratio were never swept rigorously. For a paper whose core claim is about layer placement and cache design, this is the weakest point.
  • Limited scale: 11B, limited context length, no 3D parallelism.
  • No RL. The whole pipeline is next-token SFT, which — in their words — “can only imitate the pace baked into the synthetic data”. The model does not learn when to speak from reward; it copies the timing distributions of Phase B.

What they plan next

  • Build a decision-level latency benchmark.
  • Optimise the reply/silence policy with RL, reward R = accuracy − λ · delay, where λ tunes the accuracy/timeliness trade-off.
  • Exploit channel separation for vision-K/V-only compression and quantisation.

The whole paper in five lines

  1. Problem: streaming video LLMs stop watching while they talk, so the earliest possible correction is one reply late.
  2. Architecture: keep vision out of the autoregressive sequence — in a separate cache read by 8 of 40 cross-attention layers — so an arriving frame is a cheap append that the next token already sees.
  3. Fix the missing time signal: add RoPE to cross-attention, one frame = one position.
  4. Policy: add <|silence|> (gated at p ≥ 0.6) and a turning-point token, so continue/pivot/stop is decided at every token.
  5. Data: 836 K synthetic samples where the question is fixed and the answer is rewritten as the stream advances — including replies deliberately truncated mid-sentence.
