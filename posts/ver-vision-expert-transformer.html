VER — Vision Expert Transformer for Robot Learning

Paper: Yixiao Wang, Mingxiao Huo, Zhixuan Liang, Yushi Du, Lingfeng Sun, Haotian Lin, Jinghuan Shang, Chensheng Peng, Mohit Bansal, Mingyu Ding & Masayoshi Tomizuka · ICLR 2026 · arXiv:2510.05213 · project page. Reading log — the questions I actually asked while going through Figure 2 and Equation 3.

📄 What the paper does

Distill three vision foundation models (DINOv2, ViT, CLIP) into one shared pool of MoE experts instead of into one merged representation. Then for a robot task, freeze everything and train only a small router (< 0.4% of parameters) that picks which experts to use.

Theia:  3 teachers → one fixed unified representation → policy
VER:    3 teachers → 18 experts (6 per layer × 3 layers) → router picks 2 per token → policy

How does the distillation from the three teachers work?

The important point: the experts are not one-per-teacher. There is one shared pool of 6 experts per MoE layer. What is per-teacher is the router.

  • L = 6 experts per MoE layer, K = 2 active, N = 3 MoE layers.
  • There are I = 3 Teacher-Specific (TS) routers per layer: R_dino, R_vit, R_clip.
  • One image goes through the BVT once → z.
  • The VEL then runs three times on the same z, once per teacher router. Each run picks its own 2-of-6 experts, so you get y_dino, y_vit, y_clip.
  • Each y_i goes through its own small CNN head h_i and is matched to that teacher's real feature map.
  • Teachers can share experts. Figure 4 shows ViT uses fewer experts, DINOv2 and CLIP spread over more.

Loss (Eq. 2), per teacher, averaged with alpha_i = 1/3:

L_distill = (1/3) * sum_i [ 0.9 * cosine(h_i(y_i), t_i(x)) + 0.1 * smoothL1(h_i(y_i), t_i(x)) ]

Routing itself (Eq. 1) is noisy top-K gating: the router MLP outputs both a score s1 and a noise scale s2, adds Gaussian noise, softmax, keeps the top-2, weights the 2 expert outputs by their probabilities.

If two experts are active, how do I merge them into one?

You don't pick one — you add them with weights. The router probability is the weight.

y = sum over all L experts of  R(x,l) * E_l(x),    R(x,l) = m_l * p_l

m_l is 0/1 (top-K mask), so the 4 inactive experts multiply by 0:

y = p_a * E_a(x) + p_b * E_b(x)

Small example, one patch token, softmax over 6 experts:

p = [0.05, 0.40, 0.10, 0.25, 0.15, 0.05]     top-2 = expert 2 and expert 4
m = [0,    1,    0,    1,    0,    0   ]

E2(x) = [ 2, 0, -1]        (toy 3-dim instead of 768)
E4(x) = [ 0, 4,  1]

y = 0.40*[2,0,-1] + 0.25*[0,4,1] = [0.8, 1.0, -0.15]

One vector out, same shape as one expert's output. It goes back in as the FFN output: x_out = x + y.

  • It is per token, not per image. Each of the 196 patches picks its own 2 experts. Densely that is weights [B, 197, 6] × expert outputs [B, 197, 6, 768] → sum over the expert axis → [B, 197, 768]. Real code gathers tokens per expert and scatter-adds instead.
  • The weights do not sum to 1 (0.40 + 0.25 = 0.65). That is on purpose in Shazeer noisy top-k: the leftover mass means "the router was not confident", and it is how the router gets gradient. Switch / Mixtral renormalize to 0.615 / 0.385; the paper's Eq. 1 does not.
  • Merging is safe because every expert is an MLP 768→1536→768 reading the same x, so they all live in the same space.
  • With Curriculum Top-K Annealing, training starts at K = 6 (full soft mixture) and anneals to K = 2. Proposition 1 is the reason: inactive experts are multiplied by 0, so they get no real gradient.
Does N have to equal the number of teachers?

No. They just happen to both be 3 here.

The only place I shows up in the architecture is the router count:

number of TS routers = N × I = 3 layers × 3 teachers = 9

Add SAM as a 4th teacher: I = 4, N stays 3 → 12 routers, and the table in Eq. 3 becomes 4×6. Make the last 6 layers MoE: N = 6, I stays 3 → 18 routers, and L_mi sums over 6 layers. And L = 6 is not "2 experts per teacher" — the pool is shared, and the MI loss is what pushes teachers apart.

So each layer selects its own 2 experts for each teacher?

Yes — and one level further: per layer, per teacher, and per token.

The 6 experts in layer 1 are different MLPs from the 6 in layer 2 (N × L = 18 separate expert MLPs), and the choice restarts at every layer:

teacher DINOv2, one patch token:
  layer 1:  picks E2, E5   (of layer 1's six)
  layer 2:  picks E1, E3   (of layer 2's six)
  layer 3:  picks E4, E6   (of layer 3's six)

full pass for one teacher:
  z  = BVT(image)                                  [B, 197, 768]
       ↓ layer 1: R_dino^1 → top-2 of 6 → weighted sum
       ↓ layer 2: R_dino^2 → top-2 of 6 → weighted sum
       ↓ layer 3: R_dino^3 → top-2 of 6 → weighted sum
  y_dino                                           [B, 197, 768]

Router input granularity is a single patch (Table 7), so patch 5 may take experts {1,4} while patch 90 takes {2,3}. One image, one teacher = 3 × 196 = 588 top-2 decisions.

This is also why the MI loss is computed per layer and then summed: L_mi = −( I(I;E¹) + I(I;E²) + I(I;E³) ). Three separate 3×6 tables — layer 1 can be balanced while layer 3 is collapsing.

Equation 3 — how do you compute the joint and the marginal?
L_mi = - sum_n I(I ; E^n)
     = - sum_n sum_l sum_i  p(I_i, E_l^n) * log[ p(I_i, E_l^n) / ( p(I_i) * p(E_l^n) ) ]

Two random variables: I = which teacher (3 values), E^n = which expert at layer n (6 values). Per layer you build a small 3 × 6 table. Appendix D.2 gives the recipe:

  1. Prior over teachers: p(I_i) = 1/I = 1/3, assumed uniform.
  2. Conditional: p(E_l^n | I_i) = the softmax routing score of teacher-router i for expert l at layer n. In practice averaged over all patch tokens and the batch, so each row sums to 1.
  3. Joint: p(I_i, E_l^n) = p(I_i) · p(E_l^n | I_i) = (1/3) · router_i(l). The whole table sums to 1.
  4. Marginal: p(E_l^n) = sum over i of the joint = column sums = average usage of expert l across the three teachers.

Then sum over the 3 layers and negate. Weight in the total loss: gamma = 0.0005 (Eq. 4).

I(I ; E^n)                                            scalar

Conditional vs joint — the difference is only the normalization, because p(I) is uniform here. The conditional answers "given that the teacher is DINOv2, how does it spread over the 6 experts" — three separate distributions, straight from the router. The joint answers "pick a random sample, how likely is this (teacher, expert) pair" — one distribution over all 18 cells. joint = (1/3) × row. MI needs the joint because it is an average weighted by how often each pair actually happens; using the conditional as the weight would make the total 3× too big. If teachers were weighted unevenly, say p(I) = [0.5, 0.3, 0.2], each row would shrink by a different amount and the two tables would no longer be a simple rescale.

A cleaner toy case (3 teachers, 3 experts, natural log) showing what the loss wants:

DINOv2: [0.8, 0.1, 0.1]     marginal = [0.333, 0.333, 0.333] → H(E)   = ln 3 = 1.099
ViT:    [0.1, 0.8, 0.1]     H(E|I) = entropy of [0.8,0.1,0.1] = 0.639
CLIP:   [0.1, 0.1, 0.8]     MI = 1.099 - 0.639 = 0.46 nats  → contributes -0.46 to L_mi

Both failure modes give MI = 0: all three routers flat and identical (H(E) high but H(E|I) equally high → no specialization), or all three collapsing onto one expert (H(E) = 0). That is the decomposition the paper stresses — maximize H(E^n) for load balancing, minimize H(E^n | I) so each teacher keeps its own small predictable expert subset and the three teachers' gradients do not fight inside the shared pool.
