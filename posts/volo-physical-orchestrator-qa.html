The setup, so the answers make sense. VoLoAgent is a VLM (Claude Opus 4.6) acting as the boss. It calls three families of tools: a VLA (π0.5), perception models (SAM3, Molmo2, GroundingDINO), and hand-written grasp/place primitives. The arm runs at 15 Hz. The VLM looks at a camera image and thinks at 0.2 Hz — once every 5 seconds — and each cloud call costs 1–5 s of latency. Everything below follows from that speed gap.

① The timing problem

What is (P3) Safety-aware idling?

It solves a pure timing problem. The arm runs at 15 Hz, so one step is 0.067 seconds. One VLM call takes 1 to 5 seconds. So while the VLM thinks "the robot grabbed the wrong object, what now?", the robot would execute another 15 to 75 steps.

That leaves two bad options:

  1. Let the robot keep going. It keeps carrying the wrong object and drops it in the wrong bin. Now you have a second failure to fix on top of the first.
  2. Kill the running tool. The arm may freeze mid-motion in a bad pose, or open the gripper and drop the object on the floor.

P3 is the third option: hold the robot still and keep it safe while the VLM thinks. Concretely (Sec. 4.2, "Recovery"): when the monitor returns recovery, the in-flight action chunk is halted and the arm idles — holding its current pose and current gripper state. Then the VLM enters the slow deliberation context and picks what to do next (continue / replan / rewrite / grasp / place). In their API the "do nothing" tool slot is literally called noop.

Why "safety-aware" and not just "stop". For their setup — a fixed-base Franka bolted to a table — idling is just stopping; nothing bad happens. They state the design choice in general form because for other robots stopping is not safe. A walking humanoid that freezes falls over. A mobile base that freezes in a doorway blocks it. Such a robot must keep acting in order to stay still and safe. They admit in Limitations that only the easy version is implemented:

"Safe idling currently reduces to halting the arm, which does not generalize to embodiments that must act to stay safe (e.g., a balancing humanoid)."
One line: P3 = freeze the arm to buy thinking time for a slow cloud VLM. The requirement is general; the implementation here is a stop command, and the paper says so.

② GraspGen vs the VLA

What is GraspGen, and what does "geometry-grounded motion but rigid under contact-rich interaction" mean?

GraspGen (Murali et al., 2025, NVIDIA) is a diffusion model that generates grasp poses. Input: a 3D point cloud of an object. Output: a set of 6-DoF gripper poses (position x, y, z + rotation) with a score for each, meaning "put the gripper here and close it, and you will probably be holding this object." It is a pure geometry model — it does not know the task, does not read language, does not run a control loop.

IK = inverse kinematics. GraspGen gives a pose in world space; IK converts it into the 7 joint angles the Franka must move to. VoLo uses a damped least-squares solver with several random starting guesses ("multi-start IK") so it does not land in a bad solution.

The full grasp(target) pipeline (Appendix B.2), where target is a text phrase the VLM wrote, like "the lemon":

  1. GroundingDINO or SAM3 finds "the lemon" in the RGB image → a mask.
  2. Use the depth image to cut out just that object's points → a small point cloud.
  3. GraspGen takes that point cloud → a 6-DoF grasp pose.
  4. Multi-start IK → joint angles.
  5. Build a straight-line Cartesian trajectory, stream it in the same chunk format the VLA uses.

Now the sentence itself, in two halves:

  • Geometry-grounded — the good part. The whole chain sits on real 3D measurements (mask + depth + point cloud). So it aims very accurately, and it picks the object you named, not the object that merely looks familiar. This is exactly what the VLA is bad at: π0.5 grabbed an orange when asked for a lemon (Fig. 4).
  • Rigid under contact-rich interaction — the bad part. The plan is computed once, up front, then executed open-loop with no feedback after the fingers touch. So the moment reality differs from the point cloud it has no answer: the object slides when touched, a finger catches the bowl rim, one attempt nudges the object 2 cm away. A human would wiggle and re-try. This pipeline just runs its trajectory and fails.

That is why VoLoAgent (No VLA) "struggles with contact-rich picks and exhausts the step budget" (Fig. 4).

One line: GraspGen answers where, perfectly, once — and has nothing to say about what happens after you touch the thing.
If seeing the object already gives you the action, why do you still need the VLA? And how are the two connected?

The instinct is right for one thing: seeing the object does tell you where to put the gripper. But that is not the same as doing the task.

What GraspGen actually outputs is one static pose. Notice what is missing from that output:

  • No time. It is a snapshot, not a movement. There is no "and then what."
  • No re-try. If it fails, GraspGen has nothing more to say — you would re-run the whole pipeline from a new photo.
  • No task knowledge. It knows "this is a good way to hold a mug." It does not know "hold it by the handle because we will pour later," or "lift straight up, not sideways, because it is wedged."
  • It trusts the point cloud. A depth camera sees only the front surface; the back is guessed. If the lemon is half-hidden behind the orange, the point cloud is a fragment and the pose comes out wrong.

And after GraspGen, IK builds one trajectory that the arm then runs blind — no camera, no feedback, until it finishes. Real contact is messy: the lemon rolls 2 cm when a finger brushes it and the pre-computed pose now points at empty air; a finger catches the bowl rim and the arm keeps pushing along its planned path; the grasp is off-center and the object squeezes out.

The VLA is a policy, not a pose generator. Every 1/15 second π0.5 takes fresh camera images plus the language instruction and outputs the next few joint moves — it re-decides continuously. Trained on real robot data, it learned the messy contact behaviors: the small wiggle, the re-grasp after a slip, the adjust when a finger snags. Its weakness is the mirror image: it grounds language badly, because in training a round yellow-ish fruit near a bowl usually meant "grab the thing that looks like this."

They fail in opposite directions. That is the whole design.

One concrete timeline. Task: "put the lemon in the bowl." There is also an orange on the table.

  1. t = 0 s. VLM plans subgoals. Calls vla("pick up the lemon and put it in the bowl"). π0.5 starts at 15 Hz.
  2. t = 5 s. VLM monitor tick. It looks at the front camera. The gripper is closing on the orange. It returns recovery.
  3. The in-flight VLA action chunk is halted. The arm holds still — this is P3 idling — while the VLM does its slower thinking call.
  4. VLM decides: call grasp("the lemon").
  5. The primitive runs: SAM3 masks the lemon → depth crops the point cloud → GraspGen gives a 6-DoF pose → multi-start IK → a Cartesian trajectory.
  6. The arm drives to the lemon and closes. Say the grasp fails — the lemon rolls, the fingers slip. The primitive has no answer; it is finished.
  7. But look at the state it left behind. The gripper is directly above the lemon, fingers a centimeter away, camera pointed straight down at it, and the orange is out of the picture.
  8. VLM calls vla("pick up the lemon") again. Now the VLA's weakness does not matter — there is nothing left to confuse it with. Its strength does: it closes, adjusts on contact, lifts.
"even a failed grasp leaves the gripper near the target with a clean view for the VLA to finish the pick" — Sec. 4.2

So the grasp primitive is used as an aiming tool, not a grasping tool. A failed grasp is still useful, because it moved the robot to the right place.

The mechanical connection. Both tools emit the same thing: an action chunk of 8 steps, each step 7 joint positions plus 1 binary gripper command, streamed at 15 Hz. Appendix H says the primitives "emit chunks of the same shape so the eval client treats them identically to VLA outputs." The robot cannot tell which tool produced a chunk — which is why the VLM can swap between them mid-task without anything resetting.

And neither one alone is enough (Table 2): primitives only → 17.76; VLA only → 34.97; both → 41.80. If GraspGen alone were sufficient, the first row would be high. Those runs instead "exhaust the step budget" on contact-rich picks.
One line: GraspGen picks the right object; the VLA handles the right motion. They share one action-chunk format, so the VLM can cut one off and start the other mid-task.

③ Reading the numbers

In Sec. 5.3, how can π0.5 "recover" by itself? It is just a VLA.

This looks strange, and the answer is that "recovery" is a label on the world state, not a skill the model has.

Per Appendix K.1, a separate detector program watches the simulator's ground-truth state every step and fires events by fixed rules. Nobody asks π0.5 anything.

  • wrong_object_picked fires when the gripper holds an object that is not a target of the current sub-task.
  • stuck fires after ~10 s with no end-effector progress.
  • recovery fires when "a previously-fired failure's underlying condition has resolved."

Two concrete ways that happens with no reasoning at all:

  • Stuck. π0.5 pushes against the table edge for 10 s → stuck fires. It is a closed-loop policy: every step it gets a fresh image and outputs a new action. Ten seconds later the image has changed enough that it outputs something different and the arm moves again. Condition resolved → recovery. The policy never "knew" it was stuck.
  • Wrong object. π0.5 grabs the orange instead of the lemon → WOP fires. Then the grasp slips, or the policy releases and reaches again. Condition resolved → recovery. Luck plus reactivity, not diagnosis.

There is also a bookkeeping rule that inflates the count:

"Successful episodes are post-hoc resolved as 'all recovered' (success implies every spell ended favorably even if the recovery event was cut off by the success terminal)."

π0.5 had 10 successes total and only 11 "all recovered" episodes out of 86 — so a large share of its recoveries are this rule firing on episodes that happened to end well.

The contrast is the real result. π0.5 recovers in 13% of failure episodes (11/86) by accident. VoLoAgent recovers in 54% (38/70) on purpose — because it has a monitor that detects the failure, an idle step (P3) to buy thinking time, and a menu of corrections. Same detector, same rules, both sides. 13% is the baseline for "what luck alone gives you."
How do they make sure a WOP is not just the VLM planning badly?

Straight answer: they don't separate it. WOP counts that case too, on purpose.

Per Appendix K.1, the sub-task sequence the detector uses is hand-written ground truth, not the VLM's plan:

"Each long-horizon task is decomposed into an ordered sequence of sub-tasks S1 → S2 → … → SK, where each Sk carries a set of target objects with their required end-states."

These come from gt_success_checks in the task class, authored by hand when the task was written. And the pointer moves forward by physics, not by the VLM's opinion:

"The active sub-task advances to S(k+1) once every predicate is satisfied."

The VLM saying "advance!" does not move it. Only simulator state does.

Now trace the bad-planning case. GT sub-task 1 wants the corn can back on the tray. The VLM plans wrong and says grasp("the banana"). The robot executes perfectly and picks up the banana. The detector checks: gripper holds an object that is not a target of the active sub-task → WOP fires. The robot did nothing wrong. The event fires anyway.

Why that is the right design. The two streams answer two different questions about the same episodes:

  • World failures = the symptom. "Right now, is the world in a bad state?" It does not care whose fault it is.
  • VLM failures = the cause. "Which specific VLM call was wrong?"

They are not two disjoint buckets. Both read the same gt_state stream over the same 90 episodes, and Appendix K.2 pairs each VLM call with the GT predicate it should have checked at that step. So one bad-plan moment appears twice: as WOP in the world stream, as vlm_plan_mismatch in the VLM stream. Excluding planning-caused WOPs would turn Fig. 5 from "how often is the world broken" into "how often is the world broken for reasons we chose to count."

Is bad planning actually driving the numbers? No. That is what Fig. 6 and Table 8 are for. Claude Opus 4.6, across all 90 episodes:

Four planning errors in 90 episodes. Even adding the 12 wrong-target calls gives 16 events — against 70 episodes containing failures. Sixteen bad decisions cannot be the source of 70 broken episodes. The main text says it directly: "Planning errors are rare for every backend (≤ 9 events per 90 episodes)." So the answer is empirical, not definitional — they don't prove WOP excludes planning errors, they measure planning errors separately and show the number is tiny.

The cleanest check is the baseline. π0.5 alone has no VLM at all — no planner, no monitor, no tool calls — and still hits 86 failure episodes out of 90, with 28 unrecovered WOPs. That is a control group: it proves the WOP rule fires purely from policy behavior, and it means π0.5's numbers are completely uncontaminated by VLM error.

One real soft spot. The detector enforces the GT ordering of sub-tasks. Inside a sub-task you are free — all N objects are tracked in parallel, and re-grasping an object belonging to the current sub-task is explicitly not a failure ("an in-flight re-grasp of o is part of the plan, not a failure"). But across sub-tasks, if a task genuinely allows two valid orderings and the agent picks the other one, the detector reads it as picking a non-target object. The paper does not discuss this. Most of these instructions force the order, so it is probably minor — but it is a limitation of the metric.
In Table 2, what is the "Exterior camera" row, and how is it used?

It sits under the Perception axis, so it is not a new camera. It is about which camera image gets sent to the orchestrator VLM.

The sim has three cameras, matching the real DROID cell (Appendix H):

CameraWhereWho normally uses it
Exterior ZED 2ion a tripod, off to the sidethe VLA (π0.5)
Wrist ZED minion the gripperthe VLA (π0.5)
Front ZED 2iegocentric, on the robot, looking forward at the tablethe orchestrator VLM and the grasp/place tools

Default setup (41.80). π0.5 sees exterior + wrist, as it was trained to. The VLM monitor gets the front camera at 0.2 Hz, and the grasp/place primitives consume the front camera's depth. The front view was chosen because it looks down at the table from the robot's own viewpoint, so objects are less hidden.

A nice detail in Appendix C: the front camera is flipped both left/right and front/behind relative to the robot's frame — so they paste a warning note into every prompt about the flip, otherwise the VLM says "left" when it means "right".

The ablation (36.94). Take the front camera away from the orchestrator and give it the exterior camera instead — the same view the VLA already gets. Now the whole system runs on just the two standard DROID cameras, no extra hardware. Score drops ~5 points; their explanation is that "objects are sometimes occluded in the exterior and wrist views" — from the side, the arm's own body blocks objects and objects hide behind each other, so the VLM sometimes cannot tell whether a subgoal is done.

But the drop is not statistically significant (p = 0.0942, Appendix F). This ablation and the perception-model swap are the only two rows in Table 2 that failed to separate from the full system. The practical message: the extra front camera helps a bit, but you do not need it. VoLoAgent still gets 36.94 vs 12.57 for bare π0.5 on an unmodified DROID rig — the gains come from the orchestration loop, not from special hardware.
