Self Forcing — Rolling Out on Your Own Frames

Paper: Self Forcing: Bridging the Train-Test Gap in Autoregressive Video Diffusion — Xun Huang, Zhengqi Li, Guande He, Mingyuan Zhou, Eli Shechtman (Adobe Research · UT Austin), NeurIPS 2025, arXiv:2506.08009 · project page.

✅ Conclusion

  • Training is the inference loop. Figure 2(c) is labelled “Self Forcing / AR Inference” — one picture for both. The model generates chunk by chunk on its own outputs with a KV cache, then one distribution-matching loss (DMD / SiD / GAN) lands on the whole finished video.
  • No attention mask. Causality comes from the shape of the tensor, not from a mask — the future chunks do not exist yet. That is also why it runs on FlashAttention-3 while TF/DF are stuck on FlexAttention.
  • Rolling KV cache = O(T·L) forever. A finished frame's K and V never change, so you pop the oldest entry instead of recomputing. 84 units per frame vs 525 (recompute) vs 1764 (bidirectional), with L=21, T=4.
  • Real-time is two numbers, not one. Throughput must beat the 16 FPS playback rate and first-frame latency must be short. Chunk-wise: 17.0 FPS, 0.69 s on one H100.
  • The cleanest result: CausVid and Self Forcing are identical in speed (17.0 FPS, 0.69 s) and base model. Semantic Score goes 69.80 → 81.28. The entire gap is the training method.

📄 Paper Summary

Autoregressive video diffusion models are normally trained with Teacher Forcing (denoise frame i given clean ground-truth frames before it) or Diffusion Forcing (given ground-truth frames with random noise added). Both feed the model a context it will never see at test time, so errors compound during generation — exposure bias. Self Forcing instead unrolls the model during training: each chunk is denoised conditioned on the chunks the model itself just produced, with KV caching, exactly as at inference. Because the rollout produces true samples from the inference-time distribution, the loss can be a holistic video-level distribution match (DMD, SiD, or GAN) rather than a frame-wise MSE. Two tricks keep it affordable — a 4-step diffusion backbone and a stochastic gradient truncation that backprops through only one denoising step per chunk. A rolling KV cache then extends generation to arbitrary length without recomputing anything. On Wan2.1-1.3B at 832×480 this gives 17.0 FPS with 0.69 s first-frame latency on a single H100, matching or beating models roughly 150× slower.

1. Rolling KV cache — why nothing is ever recomputed

The model is a causal transformer. Each token only looks backward. So a past frame's Key and Value depend only on that frame and older ones — never on the future. Once frame 3 is clean, its K and V are final numbers forever.

Algorithm 2 does exactly this per frame: 4 denoise passes (query = the noisy new chunk, keys/values = the cache), then 1 extra pass with the now-clean frame at timestep 0 just to make its K/V and push them into the cache. Five forwards, and that is it.

The three ways to slide a window, counted in frame-to-frame attention units with L = 21, T = 4:

Figure 3What it doesCostNumber
(a) BidirectionalNo cache possible — frame 3 looks at frame 20, so its K/V change every shift T·L²1764
(b) Causal + recomputeHas a cache, but rebuilds the overlapping frames on every window shift L² + T·L525
(c) Rolling (ours)Pop the oldest, push the newest. Never rebuild. T·L84

21× cheaper than (a), 6× cheaper than (b) — and flat. A 10-minute video costs the same per frame as the first frame. Because (b)'s rebuild is expensive, prior work slides in big jumps, which the paper notes “compromises temporal consistency” — the first frame of a new window suddenly has almost no history.

Walk it through with L = 4:

after frames 1-4:   cache = [ kv1, kv2, kv3, kv4 ]   (full)

generate frame 5:
  cache is full -> pop the oldest -> cache = [ kv2, kv3, kv4 ]
  denoise frame 5 for 4 steps, each step attends to [kv2,kv3,kv4]
  frame 5 is now clean -> 1 pass -> kv5
  push -> cache = [ kv2, kv3, kv4, kv5 ]

generate frame 6:
  pop kv2 -> [ kv3, kv4, kv5 ]
  denoise 4 steps
  push kv6 -> [ kv3, kv4, kv5, kv6 ]

... forever
The catch they had to fix. The naive rolling cache flickers badly. The first latent frame is not like the others — the causal VAE encodes it as a single image with no temporal compression, so it has different statistics, and the model saw it in context during all of training. Once the cache rolls past it, the model has never seen a world without it. Fix (§3.4): during training, block attention to the first chunk when denoising the last chunk. Measured effect on 10-second videos: recompute 4.6 FPS → rolling + this trick 16.1 FPS, without the artifacts.

2. Why noisy context frames wreck the KV cache

First, Figure 1. All three panels answer one question: to make frame i, what goes in the context?

ContextThe gap
(a) Teacher Forcingclean real frames from the dataset at test time the context is the model's own slightly-wrong frames — never seen in training
(b) Diffusion Forcingreal frames + random noise, own level per frame still real data underneath. Gaussian noise is not the same thing as model error — a melting face looks nothing like Gaussian noise
(c) Self Forcingthe model's own generated frames none — training is inference

The clean way to say it: (a) and (b) ask “given a real past, is my next frame right?”, frame by frame. (c) asks “is the whole video I just made indistinguishable from a real one?”

Now the cache. Some methods keep past frames noisy at inference to bridge the gap. Four things break:

  1. A cache entry must be final. Clean context: frame 3 is denoised once, you compute kv3 one time, done. Noisy context: frame 3 sits at noise level 500 and keeps getting cleaner as the window moves — its content changes, so its K/V change, so the cached entry is wrong and you rerun it. Cost goes right back up to the O(L²) of Figure 3(b).
  2. The timestep is an input, so the cache grows a dimension. K and V depend on (content, t), since t is injected through AdaLN. Clean context means t = 0 always → one entry per frame, 21 entries. Noisy context might need a frame at t = 250, 500, 750… → worst case one entry per (frame, timestep) pair, 21 × 4 = 84 entries, and you must pick which one to serve each step. That is the “complicates the KV-cache design”.
  3. Latency. If frame 5 is still noisy because it will be refined later, you cannot show it. You wait. That kills the whole point.
  4. Temporal consistency. Frame 6 needs to copy fine detail from frame 5 — the exact stripes on a shirt. If frame 5 in the cache is blurred by noise, those details are not there to copy, so frame 6 invents them slightly differently. Over 100 frames the shirt drifts.

And it does not fix exposure bias anyway. Adding noise makes the model less sensitive to a bad context; it does not teach it to recover from its own specific mistakes.

3. How bidirectional diffusion generates “all frames at once”

This phrase confused me. It does not mean one forward pass. It means every frame is worked on in every forward pass.

start:   frame1=noise  frame2=noise  frame3=noise  frame4=noise  frame5=noise

step 1:  put ALL 5 frames' tokens into ONE forward pass.
         full attention: frame1's tokens see frame5's tokens, and vice versa.
         output: predicted noise for all 5 -> all 5 get 2% cleaner

step 2:  same again, all 5 together -> 4% cleaner
...
step 50: all 5 frames are clean at the same moment

So “simultaneously” = all frames share the same noise level and walk down the ladder in lock step. 50 forward passes, each covering the entire video.

Bidirectional attention buys coherence: while shaping frame 1 the model already sees a draft of frame 5, so it can plan the trajectory. That is why quality is high, and exactly why it cannot stream:

  • Nothing exists until everything exists. At step 49 of 50 every frame is still a noisy mess. First-frame latency = full generation time. Wan2.1: 103 seconds.
  • Length is fixed. The whole video is one block of tokens; you cannot append frame 6 later, because frame 6 would change frames 1–5.
  • No KV cache. Past frames look at future frames, so their K/V keep changing. Nothing is reusable.

4. Rolling diffusion — why it is not strictly autoregressive

Rolling diffusion uses a progressive noise schedule: near frames almost clean, far frames almost pure noise. Each iteration the staircase shifts down one notch and a fresh noise frame enters at the back. (1000 = pure noise, 0 = clean.)

iter 1:   f1=250   f2=500   f3=750   f4=1000
iter 2:   f1=0 OK  f2=250   f3=500   f4=750    f5=1000 enters
iter 3:            f2=0 OK  f3=250   f4=500    f5=750    f6=1000 enters
iter 4:                     f3=0 OK  f4=250    f5=500    f6=750

Frames come out in order, one at a time, forever — which is why people call it autoregressive. But strict AR means p(x1) · p(x2 | x1) · p(x3 | x1,x2): to sample x2, x1 must be completely final and x3 must not exist yet. Look at iter 2 — the moment f1 became clean, f2 was already at 250 and f3 at 500. Those drafts were made before f1 was done. So f1 and f3 were shaped together. It is a joint model over the window with a clever sampler, not a chain of conditionals.

The kitchen analogy. Rolling diffusion is a chef with 4 pans on the stove at different stages. Pan 1 is ready and comes to your table. You taste it and say “make it spicy!” — but pans 2 and 3 are already half cooked without spice. Only pan 4, barely started, can be spicy. You wait three dishes. A strictly autoregressive chef has one pan on the stove: you say spicy, the very next dish is spicy.

Same thing in a game: you press jump, but the next 3 frames of walking are already drafted, so you jump 3 frames late. At 16 FPS that is ~190 ms where the game visibly ignores you. That is the paper's exact complaint — “future frames are partially pre-generated before the current frame is presented to the user… this premature commitment restricts the impact of real-time user-injected controls.”

To be fair: these methods do reduce error accumulation and do generate long videos. The complaint is specifically about the factorization and about responsiveness.

5. Algorithm 1 — what the enable/disable gradient lines do

In code it is literally torch.no_grad(). Disable means the forward pass still runs and its output is still used — only the backprop bookkeeping (graph + saved activations) is skipped.

The problem: 7 chunks × 4 steps = 28 chained forward passes. All in the graph = 28× the activations and a 28-deep chain. Their fix keeps exactly one pass per chunk in the graph.

s is sampled once per iteration (line 4, outside the frame loop), and line 7 runs j = T, …, s, so j = s is the last step for that chunk. One chunk with T = 4 and s = 2:

jbranchgradient?what it does
4elseoffdenoise from pure noise, re-noise to t3
3elseoffdenoise, re-noise to t2
2j = sONdenoise → this x̂0 is the output, appended to Xθ
—line 13offone more pass on the clean frame to make its KV

Why gradient goes off again at line 12, before caching the KV. This is a second, separate truncation: “we additionally detach the gradients of the previous frames from the current frame… by restricting gradient flow into KV cache embeddings.” If the KV pass were in the graph, the loss on chunk 7 would backprop into chunk 6's KV, then chunk 5's, all the way down — rebuilding the exact deep chain they just removed. With it detached, kv_i is a plain constant:

what the backward pass actually sees:

chunk1: [one G_theta pass] --+
chunk2: [one G_theta pass] --+
chunk3: [one G_theta pass] --+--> distribution matching loss (line 23)
   ...                       |
chunk7: [one G_theta pass] --+

no arrows between the chunks

Why s is random. If s were always 1, only the final denoising step would ever get a gradient — steps 4, 3, 2 would never learn. Drawing s fresh each iteration (s=4 → 1 step, s=3 → 2 steps, s=2 → 3, s=1 → 4) gives every step its turn. That is the stochastic in stochastic gradient truncation.

6. The attention mask — there isn't one

Figure 2 caption: Self Forcing “mirrors the AR inference process with KV caching and does not rely on special attention masks”. §4: “Self Forcing always uses full attention during training and can leverage… FlashAttention-3”.

A mask exists to hide tokens that are in the tensor but must not be seen. In TF/DF the whole video is in one forward pass, so frame 5's tokens sit next to frame 2's and must be masked. In Self Forcing, when the model works on chunk 3 the future chunks do not exist yet — the query tensor holds only chunk 3, the cache holds only chunks 1 and 2. Causality comes from the shape of the tensor.

Reading the masks off Figure 2 exactly (paper's toy setting: 3 frames, 2 tokens each):

(a) Teacher Forcing — the sequence holds two copies of most frames, a clean one (context) and a noisy one (the denoising target), so 5 blocks not 3:

                K ->   x0     x1     x0_t   x1_t   x2_t
                     (clean)(clean)(noisy)(noisy)(noisy)
  Q
  x0    (clean)        Y      .      .      .      .
  x1    (clean)        Y      Y      .      .      .
  x0_t  (noisy)        .      .      Y      .      .
  x1_t  (noisy)        Y      .      .      Y      .
  x2_t  (noisy)        Y      Y      .      .      Y

  Y = attend      . = masked out (gray in the figure)

Read the noisy rows: x0_t sees only itself (frame 0 is first, no history) and is masked from clean x0 — its own clean copy is the answer, that would be cheating. x1_t sees clean x0 + itself. x2_t sees clean x0, x1 + itself. There is no clean x2 because nothing after frame 2 needs it as context.

(b) Diffusion Forcing — one copy per frame, noisy, each with its own level ti. Plain block-causal including the diagonal:

                K ->   x0_t0   x1_t1   x2_t2

(c) Self Forcing — not a matrix at all. Figure 2(c) is three rows going down the page, one per frame, with dashed “KV caching” arrows between them. Inside each row the boxes are denoising steps, dark green → light green → white. The white blocks on the left are the cached clean past; the green block is the current chunk. Attention over that whole strip is full and dense:

Two details worth keeping: inside a chunk attention is bidirectional — the 3 latent frames of chunk 3 see each other freely, so causality is enforced at chunk granularity, not frame granularity. And the current chunk's queries see both the cached clean past and its own noisy tokens — exactly the mix the model meets at inference.

The one restriction that does exist is not a mask matrix but a window: for chunk 7 only, drop chunk 1 from the cache (28080 → 23400 tokens), which is the same operation the rolling cache performs at inference.

Why “no mask” is not just cosmetic. Self Forcing is sequential, yet its per-iteration time matches TF and DF and it converges in ~1.5 hours on 64 H100s. Two reasons: it still runs all 4680 tokens inside a chunk in parallel, and TF/DF need custom block-sparse masks which force them onto FlexAttention, while Self Forcing needs none and runs FlashAttention-3.

7. Can you feed all the chunks at once?

Training: no. Not a design choice — a data dependency. Chunk 3's context is the clean output of chunk 2, which does not exist until you have run the model on chunk 2.

chunk 1 noise --denoise 4 steps--> chunk 1 output --> kv1
                                                       |
                                                       v
chunk 2 noise --denoise 4 steps (attends kv1)--> chunk 2 output --> kv2
                                                                     |
                                                                     v
chunk 3 noise --denoise 4 steps (attends kv1,kv2)--> chunk 3 output

TF and DF can feed everything at once because their context is ground-truth data already sitting on disk. Nothing waits on anything. That convenience is exactly where exposure bias comes from — you cannot have both.

The sequential part is only over time. Everything else stays wide:

dimensionparallel?size
tokens inside one chunkyes4680, all at once
the 3 latent frames in a chunkyesfull bidirectional attention
batchyes768
chunks over timeno7, strictly one after another

Batch 768 is the trick — each sequential step is still a fat GPU-saturating matmul. You parallelize across the batch instead of across time. Per iteration the average is 7 × 2.5 = 17.5 denoise passes (s is uniform over 1–4, so 4/3/2/1 steps, average 2.5) plus 7 KV passes ≈ 25 sequential forwards against TF's 1 — and it still measures comparable, paid for by the 4-step backbone, gradient truncation, batch 768, and the missing mask.

Inference: split the question in two.

  • Pre-sampling all the noise — allowed, and mathematically identical, since Gaussian noise per chunk is independent. But pointless, and it throws away the best property: in a streaming app you do not know M in advance. Algorithm 2 samples inside the loop.
  • Denoising the whole video at once — impossible. To denoise chunk 3 you need kv2, and Algorithm 2 line 9 builds that from the fully clean chunk 2. Feeding all 7 noisy chunks together would just be bidirectional diffusion, which the causal model has never seen.

And you would not want to. One chunk at a time is why there is sub-second latency:

one chunk at a time:
  4 denoise steps -> chunk 1 is clean -> SHOW IT   <-- sub-second
  then chunk 2 -> show it
  then chunk 3 -> show it                          <-- 17 FPS streaming

whole video at once:
  ...nothing to show...
  everything appears at the end                    <-- minutes
feed all at once?why
TF / DF trainingyescontext is real data, already on disk
Self Forcing trainingnocontext is the model's own output, must be made first
Bidirectional inferenceyesfull attention, all frames share a noise level

8. VBench — the 16 dimensions and how Total is built

Quality Score — is the video nice to look at? subject consistency · background consistency · temporal flickering · motion smoothness · dynamic degree · aesthetic quality · imaging quality.

Semantic Score — did it make what the prompt asked for? object class · multiple objects · human action · color · spatial relationship · scene · appearance style · temporal style · overall consistency.

dynamic degree is an anti-cheat. A completely still video scores near-perfect on subject consistency, background consistency, temporal flickering and motion smoothness. So VBench pairs them against a metric that asks whether anything moves at all.

The weighting is not stated in the paper, but it reproduces every row of Table 1 exactly:

The honest headline is not “we beat Wan2.1 on quality” — it is “we match it while being ~150× faster in latency”, which is what the paper says.

Note the gap over Wan2.1 is much bigger to humans (62.7%) than to VBench Total (0.05 points) — the metric is not sensitive enough.

9. The streaming test — two numbers, on purpose

This is the most opinionated part of the paper, and it is a methodology argument:

“While some recent works claim ‘real-time’ video generation abilities based solely on throughput, we argue that true real-time performance requires both sufficient throughput (exceeding video playback rate) and lower latency than the perceptual threshold.”

So both numbers, both on a single H100:

  • Throughput (FPS) — must beat the 16 FPS playback rate, or you wait longer than you watch.
  • First-frame latency (s) — wall clock from “go” to the first frame on screen. For a bidirectional model this equals the entire generation time, because nothing is viewable until all 50 steps finish on all frames.

Wan2.1's 103 s → 0.69 s is the ~150× the paper quotes.

The chunk-wise vs frame-wise trade-off goes both ways. Frame-wise has better latency (0.45 s — chunk 1 is smaller, and the causal VAE makes the very first latent frame encode just one image) but worse throughput (8.9 FPS — you pay the fixed per-step overhead 21 times instead of 7).

Being straight about it: frame-wise fails the paper's own throughput test. 8.9 FPS is below the 16 FPS playback rate, so it cannot sustain live playback — it suits on-demand interactive use, not fixed-rate streaming. The paper words it softly (“suitable for latency-sensitive real-time applications”) but the number is the number. Appendix C shows the matching quality trade-off: frame-wise has higher dynamic degree but worse background consistency, motion smoothness and more temporal flickering. More AR steps, more chances to drift.

Long-video throughput is measured separately, on 10-second videos (longer than the 5 s training length): recompute-on-shift 4.6 FPS vs rolling KV cache + local-attention training 16.1 FPS. Still clears the 16 FPS bar.

What the evaluation does not cover

  1. VBench is run on 5-second videos — the training length. The long extrapolation case is never scored. The rolling-cache quality claim rests on the 16.1 FPS number and qualitative pictures in Appendix B, Fig. 7. No VBench score for long videos anywhere.
  2. The paper says so in Limitations: “quality degradation remains observable when generating videos substantially longer than those seen during training.”
  3. All speed numbers are one H100.
  4. Both speed numbers are text-to-video. The interactive control scenario motivated in the intro (games, live user input) is argued for but never benchmarked.
