Ouro — Scaling Latent Reasoning via Looped Language Models

Paper: Scaling Latent Reasoning via Looped Language Models — Zhu, Wang, Hua, Zhang, Li et al. (ByteDance Seed · UC Santa Cruz · Princeton · Mila · CMU · M-A-P), arXiv:2510.25741. The empirical-at-scale entry in this category: the other loop papers prove what a loop could do; this one trains one on 7.7T tokens and reports what actually happens.

✅ In one line

Take one stack of layers and run the input through it 4 times with the same weights. A small gate decides, per token, when to stop. At 1.4B and 2.6B parameters this matches 4B and 8B standard transformers — and the controlled experiments show the gain is not more stored knowledge (still ~2 bits per parameter either way) but better use of the knowledge already in the weights.

The architecture is almost boringly plain

The 2.6B is the 1.4B's 24 layers duplicated to 48 — they note this upcycling is unusually smooth precisely because weight sharing already forced the layers to be interchangeable-ish.

They wanted 8 loops and could not have them

Stage 1a trained with Tmax = 8 and hit loss spikes and gradient oscillation. They cut to Tmax = 4 and raised batch size 4M → 8M to stabilize. So the depth they shipped is the depth they could train, not the depth they wanted. Worth holding onto — it comes back in the extrapolation results.

Read the axis carefully

This is parameter efficiency, not compute efficiency. 2.6B × 4 loops = 192 layer passes per token. Comparing that to an 8B model on parameter count flatters the method; a FLOP-matched table is not in the paper. The claim "2–3× efficiency" means memory footprint, which is the metric that matters for edge deployment — just not the same metric as "cheaper to run".

🚪 The exit gate, derived slowly

This is the part of the paper with the most notation and the least difficulty. Everything follows from one idea: at every loop, ask "stop here?"

Survival is a product of "no"s

λₜ = σ( Linear(h⁽ᵗ⁾) )        chance of stopping AT step t, given you reached t

Sₜ = (1−λ₁)(1−λ₂)···(1−λₜ)     chance you did NOT stop in the first t steps
S₀ ≡ 1

To survive t steps you must say "no" at step 1 and "no" at step 2 and … so the "no"s multiply.

The ≡ in S₀(x) ≡ 1. Three lines means "defined to be, always" — not "happens to equal". It is bookkeeping: at t = 0 the product has no factors, and an empty product has no meaning on its own, so they declare it 1. That makes the next formula work at t = 1 with no special case: p₁ = λ₁·S₀ = λ₁.

A full worked example, Tmax = 3

Gate outputs λ₁ = 0.2, λ₂ = 0.5. Think of a pot of probability worth 1.0 that gets sliced:

start pot = 1.0

step 1:  gate takes 20% of the pot     p₁ = 0.2 × 1.0 = 0.2      pot left 0.8  ← S₁
step 2:  gate takes 50% of what's LEFT p₂ = 0.5 × 0.8 = 0.4      pot left 0.4  ← S₂
step 3:  last step, no gate, take all  p₃ =             0.4      pot left 0

                                       total = 0.2 + 0.4 + 0.4 = 1.0  ✓

Why the sum is exactly 1

The Σ just means "add these up". It equals 1 because the model definitely stops somewhere — Tmax is a hard wall. The mechanism is a telescoping sum:

pₜ  =  Sₜ₋₁ − Sₜ            "alive before t"  minus  "alive after t"

Σ pₜ = (S₀−S₁) + (S₁−S₂) + ⋯ + (S_{T−2}−S_{T−1}) + p_T
     =  S₀ − S_{T−1} + S_{T−1}          ← because they DEFINED p_T = S_{T−1}
     =  1

The two formulas for pₜ are the same thing

Sₜ = Sₜ₋₁ · (1 − λₜ)

Sₜ₋₁ − Sₜ = Sₜ₋₁ − Sₜ₋₁(1−λₜ) = Sₜ₋₁·[1 − (1−λₜ)] = λₜ · Sₜ₋₁   ✓

check at t=2:   λ₂·S₁ = 0.5 × 0.8 = 0.4      S₁ − S₂ = 0.8 − 0.4 = 0.4
The multiply form is "the slice the gate took" — that's what you compute in code. The subtract form is "how much the pot dropped" — that's what telescopes in the proof. Same number, two viewpoints. The pot shrinks by exactly the slice taken.

Inference: one knob

CDF:   0.2 → 0.6 → 1.0
q = 0.5 :  step1 0.2 < 0.5 keep going, step2 0.6 ≥ 0.5  →  EXIT AT 2
q = 0.9 :  run all 3

q is set at deployment time, no retraining. Small q = cheap and shallow, large q = deep.

🎯 Two training stages — and a notation gap between them

Stage I: expected loss minus entropy

L  =  Σₜ p(t|x)·L⁽ᵗ⁾   −   β·H( p(·|x) )
      └─ expected task loss ─┘  └─ anti-collapse ─┘

Why the entropy term exists. Without it there is a runaway loop: deeper steps have lower loss → gradient shifts mass to later steps → later steps get more training signal → they get even better → more mass. Everything collapses onto t = Tmax and adaptive computation dies.

The KL form is the same thing. With a uniform prior, KL(p ‖ uniform) = −H(p) + log Tmax, and log Tmax is a constant that gradients ignore. The KL wording exists so they can tell the ELBO story (exit step = latent variable) and connect to PonderNet.

Why uniform and not geometric? A geometric prior (PonderNet) or Poisson-lognormal (Geiping et al.) softly prefers early exit. Uniform is depth-unbiased — it decouples "this input is hard" from any global compute preference. Appendix A backs this: geometric priors plateau at higher training loss, worse as the bias strengthens, because deep steps get starved of credit assignment.

Stage II: freeze the LM, teach the gate what actually helped

Iᵢ⁽ᵗ⁾ = max(0,  lossᵢ at t−1  −  lossᵢ at t )      "did this loop help THIS token?"
wᵢ⁽ᵗ⁾ = σ( 50 · (Iᵢ⁽ᵗ⁾ − 0.005) )                   target: continue (≈1) or exit (≈0)
improvement Itarget wmeaning
0.200.9999definitely continue
0.050.90continue
0.0050.50borderline
0.000.44lean exit — but only barely

The label can shout "continue" but only whisper "exit"

Because of the max(0, ·) clip, I bottoms out at 0, so w = σ(−0.25) = 0.438 is the floor. Even when a loop makes things actively worse, the target still says "44% continue". The label is lopsided toward running deeper. The paper describes w ≈ 0 as "recommends exiting", but 0.44 is not ≈ 0. Not discussed anywhere.

Why per token

The gate is Linear(h), and a transformer has a hidden state at every position. So one loop over a 500-token sequence produces 500 λ's. With 4 loops you get 500 × 4.

Why you want that. 
"The capital of France is Paris"

  is     →  trivial, grammar forces it.  Loop 1 already nails it.   → exit early
  Paris  →  needs a fact lookup from the weights.                   → keep looping
One exit depth for the whole sentence would either waste 4 loops on is or starve Paris. And the per-token labels are free — the LM head already gives you a cross-entropy at every position.

Stage I is written per-sequence, Stage II per-token

In Eq. 2 and 4, L⁽ᵗ⁾ is already summed over all positions and p(t|x) is conditioned on the whole x; λₜ(x) carries no position index. In Eq. 5 and 6 everything suddenly gains an i and gets averaged over M. The paper never reconciles them.

It is almost certainly notation shortcut, not a design change — Section 3 states the goal is "per token and per example", Linear(h) cannot produce one number per sequence without a pooling step they never mention, pretraining runs on packed 4K web chunks where one exit depth would be meaningless, and at generation time there is no sequence yet. Figure 5 also reports an "average exit round", which only makes sense if positions differ. So the x in Stage I should really be the prefix x₁:ℓ. They wrote the compact version because the ELBO derivation is much prettier with one latent z per x.

🧮 The entropy term is regularizing the wrong quantity

This is my main technical objection, and it is worth doing with real numbers.

The paper takes entropy for each x separately, then averages over the batch. So for every single input there is a force pulling toward (0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25). But that fights the whole point — you want easy inputs sharp at step 1 and hard inputs sharp at step 4, and both of those are sharp, so both get penalized.

Same tensor, two ways to collapse it

p : [B, M, T]  =  [2, 3, 4]

version A (paper):   entropy over T  →  [2,3]   then mean over B,M  →  scalar
version B (marginal): mean over B,M  →  [4]     then entropy over T →  scalar

Version A can barely tell the two situations apart

Its loss difference between "perfect adaptive gate" and "total collapse" is 0.05 × 0.1616 = 0.008. Against an LM loss around 2.3 that is 0.3%. It is not really detecting collapse — it is applying a flat "be less confident" pressure to everything, and punishing good behavior 2.5× harder than the marginal version would. Version B keeps the full anti-collapse strength while leaving per-token sharpness alone.

The clean reframe

H_B − H_A  =  mutual information between input and exit depth

good behavior:  1.0616 − 0.5669  =  0.4947
collapse:       0.4053 − 0.4053  =  0.0000     ← exactly zero

MI is zero precisely when depth stops depending on the input — which is the failure mode. So the objective you probably want is:

L  =  task_loss  −  β · ( H_marginal  −  H_conditional_avg )

"spread the depths across the batch, but be sharp on each token"

The paper has only the second half, with the wrong sign.

eps = 1e-8
# version A (paper)
H_A = -(p * (p+eps).log()).sum(-1).mean()

# version B (marginal)
p_bar = p.reshape(-1, T).mean(0)                 # [T]
H_B   = -(p_bar * (p_bar+eps).log()).sum()

loss = task_loss - beta * (H_B - H_A)            # mutual information version
This is exactly the MoE load-balancing pattern. In MoE you balance how often each expert is used across the batch — you never force a single token to spread itself over all experts. Ouro's entropy term is the "force each token to spread" version. By Jensen, H(mean of p) ≥ mean of H(p), so the paper's choice is the strictly harsher constraint.

Two gotchas if you implement B: padding positions must be masked out of the column mean, and under data parallel each GPU's local marginal is a noisy estimate, so you need an all_reduce on the column sums first — same as MoE balancing does. Version A needs neither. That is very likely why they chose it.
Possible receipt. Section 4.3 reports dropping β from 0.1 to 0.05 to reduce "conflicting gradients between task loss and the KL penalty". The conflict is baked into which entropy they picked.

🔬 §6.1 — how they measure "knowledge capacity" in bits

The question: does looping let the model store more facts? Following Allen-Zhu's physics-of-LMs bioS(N) setup.

Fake people, on purpose

name + 5 attributes: gender, birth date, university, major, employer
each picked at RANDOM from a fixed list, then wrapped in a RANDOM template

"Anya Briar Forger was born on October 2, 1996. She studied
 Communications at MIT. She worked for Meta Platforms."

Fake data is the point: because generation is random you know exactly how much information is in there. With real Wikipedia you never do. Random templates also stop the model cheating with surface patterns. Train to saturation — 1000 exposures.

Turning cross-entropy into bits

Suppose the university list has 300 entries. A model that knows nothing spreads its guess evenly, 1/300 each, and its cross-entropy is:

CE = −Σ (1/300)·log₂(1/300) = log₂(300) = 8.23 bits
Why log₂(300) = 8.23. log₂ answers "how many yes/no questions to pin down one of 300?". Each question doubles what you can separate: 8 bits → 256 things, 9 bits → 512 things. 300 sits between, so 8.23. It is also literally a binary search — halve 300 nine times and you are down to one; 8.23 is the exact fractional cost under an optimal code.

Note the conversion: papers compute loss in nats (natural log). ln(300) = 5.70 nats, and 5.70 / ln 2 = 8.23 bits. Same quantity, different ruler.

So "knows nothing" = 8.23 and "memorized perfectly" = 0. The gap is what got stored:

bits stored  =  raw uncertainty  −  model's cross-entropy

one person's university:   8.23  −  2.23  =  6.00 bits stored

Sum over 5 attributes × N people. With ~40 bits per person and N = 100,000, the dataset holds about 4,000,000 bits. Measure the model's cross-entropy at the attribute token positions only, subtract, add it up. That total is the capacity.

Why they sweep N as well as model size

regimewhat happenswhat you measure
N small (20K)model memorizes everything easilythe dataset's size — useless
N large (500K)model runs out of room, starts forgettingthe model's real ceiling

That is why Figure 6 has separate curves for #20k / #50k / #100k / #200k / #500k — you take the upper envelope. Models are GPT-2 style, 1M to 40M params.

The result

bits stored  ≈  2 × (number of parameters)

loop-1, 10M params  →  ~20M bits
loop-4, 10M params  →  ~20M bits      SAME LINE

4× the compute buys 0× the storage

Memory lives in the weights and is capped by weight count. Running the same weights again is re-reading the same book, not buying a second book. So "24 layers looped 4 times" is not "96 layers" for memory purposes — it is still 24 layers of memory.

Which forces §6.2: if the gain is not storage, it must be use of storage.

Limits of this experiment

Models are 1M–40M — Ouro itself is 35× bigger, and the constant could bend. bioS tests only atomic single-hop facts, so it says nothing about whether looping helps store procedures, which is arguably also knowledge. And the 2 bits/param constant is Allen-Zhu's finding reproduced here; Ouro's contribution is showing looping doesn't move it.

🔗 §6.2 — where the gain actually comes from

Two synthetic manipulation tasks, same parameter budget:

  • Mano — parse an arithmetic tree and evaluate it mod 23, with no intermediate thinking allowed. Needs (a) the modular arithmetic rules stored in weights, (b) tree parsing to compose them. Looped beats iso-parameter every time, and often beats the iso-FLOP deep baseline too.
  • Multi-hop QA — "who is the teacher of the instructor of A?". Looped models reach the same accuracy with far fewer unique training samples, and learn faster at fixed sample count.

The theory piece

latent reasoning methodsequential steps for graph reachability
Discrete CoTO(n²)
Continuous CoT (Coconut-style)O(D)
Looped / Universal TransformerO(log D)

D is the graph diameter. Their Theorem 1: a one-layer transformer looped O(log² D) times decides reachability on the combined context-plus-parametric knowledge graph.

The intuition

CoT walks the knowledge graph one hop per token — sequential. A loop does squaring of the reachability matrix — every pass doubles the horizon, so you cover distance D in log D passes. Looping is parallel search; CoT is a sequential walk.
The sample-efficiency conjecture. Expressivity does not explain why looped models learn from fewer examples. Their guess: an untied deep model must search a huge function class where every block is independent, while weight tying restricts the hypothesis class to "one repeated procedure" — which is exactly the right shape for a recursive task. Smaller realizable class → lower sample complexity. This is a conjecture in the paper, not a theorem.

🪞 Faithfulness — the nicest experiment in the paper

Setup: Quora Question Pairs ("do these two questions mean the same?"), deliberately chosen because many items are genuinely ambiguous, so a thinking process has room to change its mind.

modelprobe resultreading
Qwen3-4B-Thinking0.99 ROC AUCfinal answer readable before any thinking text → the CoT is a story told after the decision
Ouro 1.4Bprobe at end of loop i−1 does not predict the loop-i answereach pass does real new work
step-to-step agreement over 1,000 pairs (out of 1000)

A[2,3] = 551        only 55.1% of step-2 answers survive to step 3
A[2,4] = 361        only 36.1% survive to step 4
A[i,i+1] → ~1000 once i ≥ 4    (untrained depth; answers converge to a fixed point)

Disagreement is the evidence

If the latent trace were a rationalization, the answer would be locked in from step 1 and every row of that matrix would read 1000. The fact that it doesn't is what "causally faithful" means here. The reasoning substrate is the sequence of hidden states; decoding text from an intermediate step is an instrumented readout, not the mechanism.

Caveat they state themselves

The gold standard for faithfulness is intervention — edit the reasoning and check the answer changes. You cannot easily edit a latent state, so they use an observational proxy (step-by-step predictability + agreement). Weaker evidence than a counterfactual, and they say so.

❌ §4.5 — the RL section, and why both attempts failed

After SFT they ran RLVR with DAPO and GRPO on DAPO-17K. Neither beat the SFT checkpoint. The stated root cause: vLLM and SGLang get their speed from a fixed execution path, and variable depth breaks that.

Attempt 1 — off-policy rollouts (mathematically broken)

1. run all 4 steps in vLLM  →  4 sets of logits per token
2. pick the step where the exit threshold is crossed, pretend it exited there
3. update using the loss up to THAT step, discard the deeper ones

GRPO and DAPO are ratio methods. The gradient contains π_new(token) / π_old(token), and π_old must be the exact probability that produced the token. Here the token was sampled at one depth and the loss computed at another. The paper says it plainly: "tokens are produced with the final depth, and losses computed at an earlier depth."

token "7" sampled at depth 4 with prob 0.60
same token at depth 2 has prob        0.15
ratio is off by 4× before training even starts  →  clipping fires on noise, not on policy drift

Attempt 2 — fixed 4 rounds (correct, but no gain)

Force exactly 4 steps in rollout and update. No early exit, no mismatch. Training ran normally and simply never beat SFT. Their explanation: scale — 1.4B/2.6B models after 8.3M SFT examples have little headroom left.

The odd observation. Trained at fixed 4 rounds, the model still used fewer rounds at inference when that helped; they call the mechanism "unclear". My read: fixed-4 training never invokes the gate, so φ is never updated and the Stage II gate survives intact. The mild surprise is that RL moved the hidden states enough to change LM behavior but not enough to invalidate the gate reading those same states.

The deeper blocker (my analysis, not the paper's words)

1. Batching dies. vLLM batches many sequences into one pass, often with a compiled static CUDA graph. If token A exits at loop 2 and token B needs loop 4, they no longer share a compute path — you either pad everyone to 4 (losing the savings) or split the batch (losing throughput).

2. The KV cache gets holes. If position 5 exits after loop 2, no loop-3 or loop-4 key/value is ever written there. Later, position 9 running its loop 3 needs to attend back to position 5's loop-3 cache — which does not exist. And you cannot substitute a shallow entry: their own Table 14 shows first-step-only reuse collapses GSM8K from 78.92 to 18.73.

So Ouro-Thinking is an SFT-only model. In 2025 RLVR is where reasoning gains come from. The comparison to Qwen3 / DeepSeek-Distill is fair (also distillation-heavy), but the reported numbers are LoopLM's SFT ceiling, not its real ceiling — and nobody has shown LoopLM can absorb RL at all. The fix is probably infrastructure, not a better algorithm.

Free speculative decoding

Loop s's head proposes, loop T's head verifies. Same weights, shared KV up to step s, no draft model to train. And you can run a safety screen on the draft before any token is surfaced. The recurrent structure is a native draft–verify primitive rather than an add-on.

Performance peaks at the trained depth and falls off a cliff

You cannot buy accuracy at inference by looping more. Depth is a training-time hyperparameter here, not a test-time dial — which is the opposite of what "scaling axis" implies and the opposite of what Geiping et al.'s recurrent-depth work aims for. The 2.6B Thinking model drops 37 points from T=4 to T=8.

And they could not train at 8 anyway (Stage 1a instability), so the axis is bounded from both ends: training stability caps it above, and extrapolation failure caps it at inference.
The unexplained asymmetry. Safety keeps improving through T=5–8 on HEx-PHI while task accuracy degrades. They note it and move on. One uncharitable reading: deeper extrapolation may just push the model toward refusal in general, which reads as "safer" on a harmfulness benchmark and as "worse" on everything else. The PCA plot showing benign/harmful separation improving is suggestive but not decisive.

🖊 What I take away

1. The storage/manipulation split is the real contribution

Everything else in the paper is engineering. The clean statement — looping does not add memory (~2 bits/param either way) but does add manipulation — is the thing that transfers to other work. It explains why gains concentrate on MMLU-Pro, BBH, GSM8K, MATH500 and stay flat on knowledge-heavy benchmarks, and it tells you the loop is a procedure mechanism, not a capacity mechanism.

2. The entropy term is the most fixable weakness

Conditional entropy fights the input-dependence it is supposed to enable, and by their own numbers barely separates collapse from good behavior (gap 0.008 in loss). Swapping to marginal entropy — or directly to mutual information — costs one all_reduce and is the same fix MoE load balancing already made. Cheap experiment, clear hypothesis.

3. "Third scaling axis" is not earned yet

It would be earned if performance kept rising past the trained depth. It falls. Until someone trains stably at large Tmax, or finds a depth-invariance trick (step-conditioning? relative depth embedding?), loop count is a hyperparameter you pick once.

4. RL is the blocking issue for the whole architecture

Not the algorithm — the serving stack. Variable per-token depth versus batched static execution and a complete KV cache is a genuine systems conflict, and until it is solved LoopLM ships at its SFT ceiling while everyone else compounds RLVR gains. That is a bigger risk to the research direction than any of the modeling choices.

Open threads

  • Does the 2 bits/param result hold at 1B+? Everything in §6.1 is 1M–40M.
  • Does the max(0,·) clip in the Stage II label (floor 0.44) measurably bias the gate toward over-thinking? Easy to check by symmetrizing the label.
  • Marginal vs conditional entropy — direct ablation on the 776M setup they already used in Appendix A.
