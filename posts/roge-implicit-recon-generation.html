RoGe — Reading Figure 1: tokenizers, ray maps, and the geometry adapter

Paper: Xiaolei Lang, Ze Kang, Zehao Huang & Naiyan Wang (Xiaomi EV · Northeastern University) · arXiv:2609.02847 · project page. Short note — only the pipeline diagram, not the experiments.

📄 What goes in, what comes out

You give it 6 posed images and a camera path of 81 poses. It gives back an 81-frame video walking along that path. The paper calls this roaming a scene anchored by sparse views.

The claim in the title is implicit. Other hybrid methods reconstruct the scene into something you can look at — a point map, 3D Gaussians, a rendered image — and hand that to a video model. RoGe never builds that thing. The scene stays as transformer tokens, and the diffusion model reads the tokens directly. That also means gradients can flow back, so the two halves train together.

In the figure the snowflake sits on the 3D Causal VAE (frozen). The flames sit on the camera-conditioned VGGT, the geometry adapter, the patch embeddings, and the Video DiT. The DiT and the cross-attention blocks only move through LoRA (rank 32 and rank 8).

📄 Ray maps — the same thing used two different ways

For every pixel of every view you take the ray it looks along: the origin o (the camera centre) and the unit direction d. Store the six numbers (d, o × d). That is the Plücker coordinate. Stack over all pixels and you get a 6-channel image the same size as the picture.

Why not just pass the pose matrix? A pose is one vector for the whole frame. A ray map gives every pixel its own ray, so the network keeps the link between a spatial location in the latent and the direction that location looks in. That link is what later lets the diffusion model line geometry up with the right part of the picture.

Ray maps are built for all 87 views — the 6 inputs and the 81 targets. And Figure 1 shows them leaving in two arrows, because the paper keeps two encodings of the same thing:

📄 The three tokenizers in the figure

1. Camera-Conditioned VGGT — makes the scene tokens. VGGT is a pretrained multi-view geometry transformer. RoGe adds one small thing: a 2-layer MLP turns each camera's intrinsics and pose into a 1024-dim token, which is added to VGGT's own camera token for that view. Then the tokens from the last local attention layer and the last global attention layer are glued along channels, 1024 + 1024 = 2048-dim scene tokens. It stops there — no depth map, no point cloud, no Gaussians.

Why the camera conditioning is not a detail. Plain VGGT builds features in its own frame, anchored to its first input view, at a scale it picks itself. Your target trajectory is in the frame of the poses you gave. Those two disagree, and you cannot re-express the tokens in a new frame afterwards. The ablation shows what that costs: raw VGGT tokens as conditioning score worse than feeding no geometry at all (18.50 vs 19.01 PSNR). Conditioning VGGT on the input cameras fixes it (19.35).

2. Ray Map Encoder — makes the ray tokens. One 2D convolution with 8×8 patches, one ray token per patch, for all 87 views, plus 4 learnable register tokens per view (the DINOv2 trick — give the model somewhere to dump global junk so it does not pollute the patch tokens).

Then Ray–Scene Cross-Attention is the "query" step. Scene tokens are squeezed 2048 → 768, and 12 blocks run three operations each:

Q̃ = Q + SelfAttn(Q)                 # rays talk to each other, within one view
Q ← Q̃ + CrossAttn(Q̃, Z, Z)          # rays read the scene
Z ← Z  + CrossAttn(Z, Q̃, Q̃)         # the scene reads the rays back  ← only blocks 1..11

The backward arrow is the interesting one: the scene representation gets refined by the rays that are asking about it. The last block drops it and only reads. Output is F_r, the per-view geometric features. A small RGB head decodes them for the render loss (weight 0.1) — the dashed branch going down in the figure. That loss is an anchor, not the output.

3. The 3D Causal VAE — and it is used in a split way. This is the frozen Wan2.1 video tokenizer. The problem it has to solve: 6 context views are an unordered set, 81 target frames are an ordered video. Feed them the same way and you either invent a fake ordering for the context, or you break time continuity in the output. So:

  • each context image is encoded alone, as a 1-frame video → 6 latent slots
  • the target video is encoded jointly → 1 + 80/4 = 21 latent slots

Total 27 slots. In the figure those are the clean boxes ("Clean Context Latents") and the grainy boxes ("Noisy Target Latents"). Remember 27 — the adapter exists to hit that number.

🖊 Latent-Aligned Geometry Adapter

This is the box that makes the whole idea work, and it is doing something very plain: F_r and the VAE latents disagree on three axes, and it fixes each one.

MismatchFixNumbers
channels1×1 convolution768 → 384
spatial sizebilinear resize to the latent grid→ 24 × 42
timePack487 views → 27 slots

The first two are bookkeeping. Pack4 is the one worth reading twice. VGGT produced 87 separate per-view feature maps. The VAE produced 27 latent slots. They must line up one to one, so:

6 context frames   ──► each replicated 4×  ──► 24
1st target frame   ──► replicated 4×       ──►  4
80 remaining target frames ──► unchanged   ──► 80
                                              ───
                                              108   then stack every 4 along channels
                                              108 / 4 = 27   ✓ exactly the latent slots
                                              channels 4 × 384 = 1536
The replication is not padding. It copies exactly what the causal VAE did in time — keep frame 1, then compress every 4 frames after it. That is why the name is "latent-aligned". Slot k of the geometry condition covers the same frames as slot k of the latent.

The raw ray maps get the same treatment on the right-hand path: Pack4 turns 6 channels into 24, then PixelUnshuffle8 folds each 8×8 pixel block into channels, 24 × 64 = 1536, also at 24 × 42. Nothing is thrown away — the ray information just moves from space into channels. That is what "lossless packed ray map" means.

Then the ⊕ in the figure. Three separate 3D convolutional patch embeddings, then a plain sum:

X̃ = PatchEmbed_1( [noisy latent ; appearance condition] )   # pretrained Wan weights
  + PatchEmbed_2( packed ray maps    )                      # 1536 ch, random init
  + PatchEmbed_3( geometry condition )                      # 1536 ch, random init

The appearance condition is a binary mask saying which slots are context, plus the context latents in their 6 slots and zeros in the 21 target slots. After a 2×2 spatial patch you get 27 × 12 × 21 = 6804 tokens into the DiT.

One detail that is in the text but not in the figure: temporal RoPE is switched off on the 6 context slots and kept only along the target trajectory. So the context stays permutation-invariant (a set) and the output stays a video. Same motive as the split VAE encoding.

🖊 What I take away

The contribution is the gradient path, not the adapter. The adapter is a 1×1 conv, a resize, and a replicate-and-stack. Nothing clever. What is new is that the flow-matching loss flows back through it, through the cross-attention, and into VGGT. Two-stage methods (GEN3C, NeoVerse) cannot do this — the reconstruction is finished and frozen before generation starts, so generation inherits its errors and reconstruction never hears about them.

Their ablation is the honest version of the argument, and I like that they published the row where their own idea loses:

Geometric conditionJoint trainPSNR
none (generation only)–19.01
raw VGGT patch tokens–18.50 — worse than nothing
+ camera-conditioned–19.35
rendered RGB–20.35
ray-queried features–20.59
ray-queried features✓20.92

So the ordering is: geometry in the wrong frame is actively harmful; a rendered image is already decent; querying the implicit features beats the render by a little; joint training adds the last third of a point. End-to-end is the smallest of the three gains, which is worth remembering when reading the abstract.

Things I could not pin down from the paper.
  • Equation 10 writes F_r at H/4 × W/4, but the ray encoder uses 8×8 patches, which gives H/8 × W/8. The two branches also run at different resolutions (288×504 for reconstruction, 192×336 for generation), so the resolution bookkeeping does not close cleanly for me. It does not matter downstream — the adapter bilinearly resizes anyway.
  • The VAE channel count and the DiT hidden width are not stated; I am reading them off Wan2.1-I2V-14B.
  • The scene-token count out of VGGT is never given. VGGT uses 14-pixel patches, which divides neither 288 nor 504, so there is a resize step the paper does not describe.
  • All results are on DL3DV with 10 test sequences from SEVA. That is a small evaluation set for the size of the claim.

In one paragraph: Figure 1 is two tracks meeting at a sum. The lower track turns 6 posed images into VGGT scene tokens, then lets ray tokens from all 87 views query them through 12 bidirectional cross-attention blocks. The upper track uses a frozen causal VAE that encodes context images one at a time and the target video jointly, giving 27 latent slots. The Latent-Aligned Geometry Adapter is the bridge — channels by 1×1 conv, space by bilinear resize, and time by a replicate-and-stack that maps 87 view features onto exactly those 27 slots so they can be added to the noisy latent inside the DiT patch embedding.
