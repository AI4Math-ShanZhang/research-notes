Patch Policy — Efficient Embodied Control via Dense Visual Representations

Paper: Gaoyue Zhou, Zichen Jeff Cui, Ada Langford, Bowen Tan, Yann LeCun & Lerrel Pinto (NYU Courant · Meta-FAIR · AMI Labs), July 2026, arXiv:2607.18236 · project page. Short note — the questions I actually had while reading, in plain words.

📄 Paper Summary

The idea in one line. Small robot policies should feed the ViT's patch tokens into the policy, instead of squashing the image into one vector (CLS token or average pool). Keep the encoder frozen. That's it.

Everything else in the paper is evidence for that sentence.

normal VLA:     image + text instruction → VLM (7B) → action head
Patch Policy:   images → frozen ViT (no gradient) → small transformer (~10-30M) → action head

There is no text anywhere. The task is specified by a goal image or a goal vector, not by a sentence. That is why "Goal Image / or / Goal Vector" is its own input box in Figure 2.

🖊 Key Notes

The vision encoders are DINOv2 and WebSSL — both public and frozen. So what is the contribution?

This is an empirical / systems paper, not a new-model paper. Fair to be suspicious. What it actually contributes:

  1. The main claim with controlled evidence. Hold the policy head fixed, swap only the visual input: CLS vs avg-pool vs raw patches. Cube task: CLS 0.21, avg-pool 0.21, patches 1.73.
  2. One small architecture piece — the block-causal mask. The only "method" in the paper. Honestly a known trick (video models, and the authors' own DINO-WM use it).
  3. A head-to-head benchmark of 5 frozen encoders for control. DINOv2 and WebSSL win, SigLIP 2 loses. Their reading: SigLIP 2 optimizes language-image matching and trades away the fine geometry you need to insert a plug. Genuinely useful — people usually pick an encoder by vibes.
  4. The efficiency headline. Beats fine-tuned OpenVLA-OFT (7.6B, 61.7 ms) with ~0.7–5% of the params at ~11 ms. Real Franka cable insertion, 2 mm tolerance: 0.70 vs 0.30.

The argument is: the good visual features already exist and are free — robotics just isn't using them properly. The gap is not "we need better vision", it is "the plumbing between free vision and small policies is wrong."

Where to push back. Component novelty is near zero — ACT already uses patch tokens, VLAs already use patch tokens, and the authors' own DINO-WM already used frozen DINOv2 patches for world models. This is essentially "DINO-WM, but for policies." And the OpenVLA-OFT comparison is on the VLA's weak side: VLAs are built for language following and cross-task generalization, but every task here is in-domain, single-task, no language. The paper half-admits this ("for in-domain task learning"). Also: frozen encoder only, behavior cloning only, and the "40% relative improvement" is against their own global-feature baselines.

1. Figure 2 — what the framework actually is

Concrete numbers, using their real setup (DINOv2 ViT-S/14, 224×224 image, 2 camera views, T=5):

stepshape
one image224 × 224 × 3
after frozen ViT256 patches × 384 dim
view0 + view1512 tokens × 384
over T=5 frames5 × 512 tokens × 384

Two things get combined, and they combine in two different directions:

Multiple views → stacked along the TOKEN axis (the sequence gets longer). Blue = view0's patches, orange = view1's patches, sitting on top of each other in the zt column. One timestep is 256 + 256 = 512 tokens. Nothing is repeated here, they are just appended.
Goal → stacked along the FEATURE axis (each token gets fatter). Every row of zt in the figure has two cells side by side: left = the observation patch, right = gray = the goal. So one token is [obs patch (384), goal (384)] → 768 dim.

And here is the thing that looked wrong at first — one goal image, many tokens, many timesteps. The goal features are broadcast (copied):

  • copied across all T timesteps (the goal doesn't change during the episode, so this is free)
  • copied across both views (view0's patch 5 and view1's patch 5 both get glued to goal patch 5)

The paper writes the shape as T × P × 2D. The 2D is exactly the "obs half + goal half" glue, and the fact that D doubled while T and P did not is the proof that it's a broadcast, not new tokens.

One detail worth noticing: goal patch i is paired with observation patch i — same spatial slot. So the token says "here is what patch 5 looks like now, and here is what patch 5 should look like at the end." That pairing is only meaningful because the camera is fixed. For the goal-vector case there are no patches at all, so the vector is just copied onto every token.

2. The policy side and the mask

frames:   [ z_t (512 tok) ][ z_t+1 (512 tok) ][ z_t+2 (512 tok) ]  → flatten to 1536 tokens
          + learned 1D position embedding
          ↓
   8-layer transformer, dim 512, block-causal mask
          ↓
   take the LAST token of each frame as the readout
          ↓
   Action Head  →  â_t, â_t+1, ... â_t+k   (an action chunk)
          ↓
   loss vs ground truth a_t ... a_t+k

The yellow staircase in Figure 2 is the mask: lower-triangular by frame block. Frame 2 sees frames 1 and 2, not frame 3. The zoom-in shows one block fully filled — inside a single frame all 512 patches see each other freely, both directions.

Why it matters: they read the action out of the last token of each frame. If attention were not causal across frames, the readout for frame t would peek at frame t+1, so the model would cheat during training and break at test time.

3. The action head is not new

This is the part the paper deliberately borrows. The head is a plug-in slot, and they test two off-the-shelf ones:

  • VQ-BeT — a VQ-VAE over action chunks (codebook 16, 2 residual groups). The transformer predicts which code (classification) plus a small offset (regression). Fast: ~11 ms.
  • Diffusion Policy — the token conditions a denoiser that refines the action chunk. Slow: ~450 ms, and that cost is the denoising loop, not the vision.

Both unchanged. The point is that swapping global features for patch features helps either head, which is what makes the claim about representations rather than about architecture.

So the framework is three parts, and the middle one is what they added: frozen ViT (can be precomputed and cached) → small policy transformer (where the mask lives) → action head. Trainable params: ~9M (Diffusion) to ~30M (VQ-BeT), against OpenVLA-OFT's 7.6B.

The trap when reading Table 1. BlockPush and Cube are scored out of 2.0, not 1.0. So Cube "1.73" means 1.73 of 2 blocks stacked, and the CLS baseline "0.21" means it basically never finishes even one. Figure 5 normalizes to [0,1] so the plot is comparable — Table 1 does not.

What each one tests.

  • Push-T — push a green T into a fixed gray T zone, scored by final overlap. Sanity check. The goal is fixed, so no goal image is needed.
  • LIBERO Goal — 10 tasks in one scene. The scene looks the same every time, only the instruction differs. This is the one environment where the goal input really matters, and since Patch Policy has no language it has to come through the goal image. Also the only place OpenVLA-OFT's number is copied from the original paper rather than re-run.
  • BlockPush — two blocks, two targets, assignment can be same-colored or opposite-colored. So the expert data is multi-modal: two valid behaviors from the same state. That is exactly why VQ-BeT and Diffusion Policy are the heads — a plain regression head would average the two modes and push the block into the gap between targets.
  • Cube — UR5e stacks two cubes, randomized starts, 200-step budget. The training data was not a clean expert: it came from an expert policy with temporally correlated noise (OGBench's protocol), so the demos are deliberately imperfect. Hardest of the four, and where the gap is widest: patches 1.73 vs CLS 0.21 vs OpenVLA-OFT 1.50.

Details that change how you read the numbers.

  • Vision only, no proprioception — "All policies receive only visual inputs." Joint angles are not given. This makes the CLS-vs-patch comparison clean, but it also makes the global-feature baselines look worse than they would in a normal setup. Keep that in mind next to the 40% headline.
  • Context windows differ per environment (Table 9): Push-T 5 frames / 5-step chunk, LIBERO Goal 10 frames / 1 step, BlockPush 3 / 1, Cube 5 / 5. So token counts vary a lot.
  • LIBERO Goal uses a much smaller policy — 6 layers, dim 120, versus 8 layers, dim 512 for the other three. Big difference, unexplained.
  • Best checkpoint per run is selected. Common in this literature, but it inflates everyone's numbers, theirs and the baselines'.
  • A fifth simulated suite exists but is not in Section 3.1 — CAP-EgoGym (Appendix A.3): pickup, door/drawer open, door/drawer close, built on ~20k real handheld-tool demos with procedurally generated textures. The only place they test generalization rather than in-domain imitation.

5. What "100 rollouts" and "100 trials (10 per goal)" actually mean

A rollout (also called an episode, or a trial — the paper uses all three for the same thing) is: reset the sim to a fresh starting state, let the policy run until it finishes or times out, write down one score. 100 rollouts = do that 100 times and average.

"100 trials (10 per goal)" just says the 100 runs are split evenly over LIBERO's 10 tasks — 10 runs each — not 90 runs on one easy task and 10 on the rest.

LIBERO Goal, score 0.98    (each run is pass/fail)
  98 succeeded, 2 failed
  (98×1 + 2×0) / 100 = 0.98

Cube, score 1.73           (each run scores 0, 1, or 2 cubes placed)
  75 runs placed 2 cubes
  23 runs placed 1 cube
   2 runs placed 0 cubes
  (75×2 + 23×1 + 2×0) / 100 = 173 / 100 = 1.73

Push-T, score 0.80         (each run already gives a fraction)
  run 1: 0.91,  run 2: 0.66,  run 3: 0.88,  ...
  average of the 100 numbers = 0.80
Where the ± comes from. In Table 1, 1.73 ±0.02 — that spread is not across the 100 rollouts. It is across 3 random seeds: 
seed 1: train a policy → 100 rollouts → 1.71
seed 2: train a policy → 100 rollouts → 1.73
seed 3: train a policy → 100 rollouts → 1.75
reported: 1.73 ±0.02   ← std over these 3 numbers
So each cell is really 300 rollouts and 3 separately trained models. A small ± means "retraining is stable", not "the policy behaves the same every episode." Some rows have no ± at all (DynaMo, OpenVLA-OFT) — single runs or copied numbers, so you can't tell how noisy they are. And 3 seeds is a small sample; don't read ±0.02 as a hard confidence interval.

The real robot is different: only 20 trials per task, one model, no seeds, because a human has to reset the table by hand each time. A real-robot 0.70 vs 0.60 is 14 successes vs 12 out of 20 — a 2-episode difference. Treat those gaps with more caution than the sim ones.

6. Are the 100 runs the same scenario, or different arrangements?

Different arrangement each run — but the same scene and the same objects. Only placement changes.

What the paper states explicitly. Only for Cube: "the initial positions of the cube blocks are randomized." And for the real tasks: Cable Insertion — cable start pose randomized in a 2 cm × 2 cm square, socket fixed; Pen Collection — 3 pens permutable across trials, each jittered ±1 cm and ±15°.

What it leaves unstated. Push-T, LIBERO Goal and BlockPush get no randomization description at all — they inherit the benchmark defaults. From those benchmarks: Push-T re-draws the T-block pose and pusher start each episode; BlockPush varies block positions and which target each block goes to; LIBERO ships a fixed pool of saved initial states per task and evaluation draws from that pool (varied, but from a predefined list).

What never changes across the 100 runs: the objects, the background, table, lighting, camera positions, the goal configuration, the robot. "Different arrangement" means the same stuff in different places. Nothing new ever appears.

So these 100 runs measure precision and robustness to placement — can the policy handle a cube 5 cm left of where it saw one in training. They do not measure generalization — a new object, a different table, different lights.

That is exactly why the OpenVLA-OFT comparison is lopsided. A 7B VLA carries huge visual and language priors that buy generalization. If you only test placement variation inside one fixed scene, those priors never get to pay off, and the VLA is just a slow way to do something small.

The paper does have one real generalization test, but it's in the appendix: Franka Pickup (A.3), 10 objects never seen in training, 10 trials each = 100 trials; plus CAP-EgoGym with procedurally generated textures. Those are the only places where new stuff appears.
