LayerNorm as Implicit Gain Control in Looped Transformers

Paper: LayerNorm as Implicit Gain Control in Looped Transformers — Matthias M. M. Buehlmaier (University of Hong Kong), arXiv:2607.10681. July 2026. Code: clojure-finance/mythjure, tag paper-v1.

✅ Three things to remember

  1. LayerNorm divides by the state size. So a big state means a gentle block. And a high carry makes the state big. So turning the carry up automatically makes the block gentler. Free, automatic, nobody tuned it.
  2. Judge safety by long-run growth, not worst-case growth. The budget is 1 minus long-run growth. It falls from 0.16 to 0.01 as the carry goes to 1. Long memory costs you your safety margin.
  3. The block is the memory. The carry is just a brake. Cut the block's view of the state and accuracy drops from 1.00 to 0.09 — and the carry does not rise to fill in.

Two warnings: the danger is activations shrinking, not exploding; and watch the smallest single token, not the average.

📄 Part 1 — The notation

The loop equation

h_next = A * h + f(h, e)

That is the whole paper in one line. Six symbols. Here they are.

h — the hidden state.
This is the thing that goes around the loop. A big pile of numbers. Think of it as the model's current "working notes."
t — which loop step you are on.
You start with h_0. Run the block, get h_1. Run it again, get h_2. And so on. Say 8 steps total.
e — the input.
The encoded prompt. It gets fed in fresh at every loop step, not just the first. It never changes during the loop.
f — the block.
One transformer block: attention + MLP, with LayerNorm inside. It reads the current h and the input e, and outputs a change to add. Not a new h — just the change. Like "adjust your notes by this much."
A — the carry.
One number per channel, always between 0 and 1. It says how much of the old h you keep. If A = 0.9, you keep 90% of your old notes. If A = 0.2, you throw away 80% and mostly start fresh each step.
rho — the biggest number in A.
Just a shorthand. Instead of talking about 32 channels, you talk about the single slowest one. When I say "high carry," I mean rho close to 1.

So the line reads:

new notes = (keep some of the old notes) + (the block's suggested change)

That's it. Two paths bring information from one loop step to the next:

  • Path 1, the carry: A * h. Dumb and linear. Just multiply by a number.
  • Path 2, the block: f(h, e). Smart and nonlinear. It actually reads h and thinks about it.
flowchart LR H["h_t (working notes)"] --> C["carry: A * h
linear, dumb"] H --> B["block: f(h, e)
nonlinear, smart"] E["e (input, frozen)"] --> B C --> S(("+")) B --> S S --> H2["h_t+1"]
The paper's main question: which of these two paths is the model's real memory?

Four more symbols you need

h* — where the loop settles.
If you loop enough times and h stops changing, that resting value is h*. Called a fixed point. Read it "h-star."
|h| — the size of h.
Squash the whole pile of numbers into one number saying "how big." Like a volume knob reading.
Lip(f) — the sensitivity of the block.
If the input to the block moves a little, how much does the output move? Sensitivity 5 means the output moves 5x as much as the input did. Sensitivity 0.1 means it barely reacts.
This is the key symbol. High sensitivity = the loop is jumpy and can blow up. Low sensitivity = the loop is calm and settles.
F — the size of the block's output at the settled point.
So F = |f|. In their runs this sat around 25 and barely moved. LayerNorm is why it barely moves.

🖊 Part 2 — The story, one step at a time

Step 1. LayerNorm divides by the size of the state

That's all LayerNorm does that matters here. It divides.

So if h gets bigger, the block's sensitivity gets smaller. Here is what they measured:

Read the table: when h was tiny (2), the block was wildly jumpy (17). When h was big (218), the block barely reacted (0.12). And the last column — the two multiplied together — is almost the same number every time. So:

sensitivity = C / (size of h) where C is about 27

That constant C is the first real finding. Sensitivity is not free to be anything. It is locked to the size of h.

Step 2. The carry decides how big h settles at

When the loop has settled, nothing changes anymore. So whatever the carry throws away, the block must put back. Exactly.

The carry throws away (1 - rho) of the state each step. The block puts back F. Setting them equal:

(1 - rho) × |h*| = F |h*| = F / (1 - rho)

With F about 25, plug in numbers:

So a high carry forces a big state. Makes sense — you're keeping more each round, so more piles up.

Step 3. Chain Step 1 and Step 2 together

This is the actual finding of the paper.

  • Step 1 said: sensitivity = C / (size of h)
  • Step 2 said: size of h = F / (1 - rho)

Substitute the second into the first:

sensitivity = (C / F) × (1 - rho)

They measured C / F at about 2.5, and it stayed about 2.5 across every carry they tried. So sensitivity ≈ 2.5 × (1 - rho). Here it is with numbers:

Read this out loud: when you turn the carry up, the block automatically becomes gentler.

Nobody programmed that. Nobody tuned it. It happens because turning the carry up makes h bigger, and LayerNorm divides by the size of h.

That is what "implicit gain control" means. Gain = sensitivity. It gets controlled on its own.

Step 4. But this is not enough to keep the loop safe

You'd hope the two paths add up to less than 1, so each step shrinks. Add them:

carry + sensitivity = rho + 2.5 × (1 - rho) rho = 0.3 → 0.3 + 1.75 = 2.05 rho = 0.9 → 0.9 + 0.25 = 1.15 rho = 1.0 → 1.0 + 0.00 = 1.00

All above 1. This sum never says "safe" — except right at rho = 1, which is useless.

And yet the loop does settle in practice. So this sum is measuring the wrong thing.

Step 5. Two different ways to measure "growth per step"

Here is the piece that makes the whole thing work.

  • Worst-case growth (the paper says "operator norm"): push the state in the single worst direction. How much does it grow in one step?
  • Long-run growth (the paper says "spectral radius"): run many steps. On average, per step, does it grow or shrink?

These are not the same number. They measured both:

rhoworst-case growthlong-run growth
0.301.740.92
0.701.300.96
0.951.020.99

Look at the first row. Worst-case is 1.74, so a bump can get 74% bigger in one step. But long-run is 0.92, so it shrinks 8% per step over time.

Meaning: a disturbance grows first, then dies. It bulges, then fades. That's fine. The loop is safe.

Why can these differ? Because the two paths push in different directions. The carry is diagonal — it only stretches along the channel axes. The block is dense — attention mixes channels together. They don't line up, so the worst case never actually happens twice in a row.

budget = 1 - long-run growth

Step 6. The price of memory

Now use that budget formula on their numbers. Averaged over 10 random starts:

From 0.159 down to 0.012. Thirteen times thinner.

High carry means information survives longer across loop steps. It also means almost no safety margin. You cannot have both.

Also: out of 10 random starts, 1 or 2 never settled at all. They just circled forever. That happened even at low carry.

This is the "necessary but not sufficient" line. Forcing rho < 1 controls the dumb linear path. It says nothing about the smart nonlinear path. So rho < 1 does not guarantee the loop converges.

Step 7. The danger is the opposite of what you'd guess

Go back to the Step 1 table:

  • h big (218) → sensitivity 0.12 → calm, safe
  • h small (2) → sensitivity 17 → jumpy, dangerous

So the thing that kills you is h shrinking, not exploding. If activations collapse toward zero, LayerNorm's division blows the sensitivity up, the loop stops settling, and gradients explode. Explosion caused by things getting too small.

One more wrinkle: LayerNorm works per token. So one single collapsed token is enough to spike the sensitivity, while the overall average still looks perfectly healthy.

What to watch: the smallest per-token spread. Not the global average.

Step 8. Which path is the actual memory? (the ablation)

Back to the main question from Part 1. Two paths — carry, or block. Which one carries memory across loop steps?

The task. A sequence like [A] [pad] [pad] [pad] [?]. Answer A at the ?. The gap is k positions.

The trick to make it hard. They restricted attention so each position can only see itself and the one before it. Now info must crawl one position per loop step. You genuinely need multi-step memory.

In run B they cut one wire: the block can no longer see h. It only sees the input e. So the carry becomes the only path carrying anything across loop steps.

  • A vs B: one wire changed. 1.00 falls to 0.09. So the memory was flowing through the block.
  • B vs C: same cut block, but now let attention see everything. Back to 1.00. This proves B didn't fail because the model got weaker. It failed because it lost the specific ability to pass info across loop steps.

And the carry did not step up to help. In run B it sat at 0.522, basically where it started. It had every reason to rise and take over the job. It couldn't.

They also checked the other direction: as the gap k grew from 2 to 6, the carry should have climbed toward 0.83 if it were doing the memory job. It went down instead — 0.524 to 0.486.

How to actually run this ablation

It is one line. The block's input is normally h + e. You feed it just e.

def block_increment(h, e, see_h=True):
    x = (h + e) if see_h else e      # <-- the whole ablation
    out = transformer_block(x)       # pre-LN attention + MLP
    return out - x                   # return only the CHANGE

A = torch.exp(-torch.exp(log_A))     # forces A into (0, 1)
h = torch.zeros_like(e)
for t in range(T):                   # T = 8
    h = A * h + block_increment(h, e, see_h)

Two details in that snippet. One: the block returns out - x, not out. A transformer block already has its own residual, so returning out would give you the identity twice — once inside the block, once from the carry — and the loop would blow up. Two: log_A passed through a double exponential is what forces the carry into (0, 1) by construction.

Why the cut is so clean. e never changes during the loop. So if the block only sees e, it produces the same output at every step. You can hoist it out:

c = block_increment(None, e, see_h=False)   # compute ONCE
for t in range(T):
    h = A * h + c                           # c is a fixed constant

The loop became multiply-and-add. With A = 0.5 and c = 10, starting at 0: 10, 15, 17.5, 18.75 … sliding toward 20. That's all it can ever do. It cannot move information from position 3 to position 7. It is a leaky bucket filling up. Hence 0.09 — basically guessing.

And that is exactly why run C rescues it: with full attention, the one-time computation of c already has the answer inside it before the loop even starts.

The exam analogy. e is the question sheet — printed, on the desk, never changes. h is your scratch paper — you update it each round. Normal: each round you look at the question and your scratch paper, so round 3 builds on round 2. Ablated: you may only look at the question sheet. So every round you have the identical first thought and write it down again. Eight rounds, zero progress. The carry is your scratch paper slowly fading — but nobody is reading it. Faded notes nobody reads don't help.

If you run it yourself

  • Keep everything else identical. Only see_h changes between A and B; only the attention mask changes between B and C.
  • Train from scratch each time (the paper says "cold start"). Fine-tuning B from A's weights measures forgetting, not capability.
  • Leave the carry trainable. You want it to have the chance to take over. The fact that it doesn't is the result. Freezing it would prove nothing.
  • Verify the cut is real: in ablated mode the increment must be byte-identical at every loop step. assert torch.allclose(increments[0], increments[-1]). If that fails, h is leaking in somewhere.
  • Their settings: gap k=4, T=8 loop steps, d=24, attention window 1.

Step 9. The one case where the carry does get used

They built a task where each channel just needs to remember its own value, decaying at its own rate. No mixing between channels. That is exactly, literally what a carry does.

There the carry moved, and it matched the target rates at correlation 0.92.

Why this experiment matters: without it you couldn't tell "the carry is not needed" apart from "the gradient is broken." This proves the gradient works fine. The carry just doesn't get used for normal tasks.

Language tasks always mix channels. So for language, the carry stays idle.

⚠ How much to trust it

  • Single author, business faculty, not an ML lab. Hours on a consumer laptop.
  • d = 8 to 32, at most 4 heads, sequence lengths up to 32, CPU only, written from scratch in Clojure with hand-derived backprop (verified against finite differences to 1e-6).
  • The six "tasks" are Tiny Shakespeare plus toy synthetics.
  • The stability tables use random untrained weights. Those growth and budget numbers describe the model at initialization, not after training. The paper says so.
  • The math in the appendix is clean and doesn't depend on dimension — the coupling follows from the normalizer ignoring scale, so it holds for RMSNorm too. Post-LN would not get it.

Verdict: take the mechanism seriously, treat the numbers as a small-scale illustration.

🔗 Hooks to the rest of this category

  • Ouro's exit gate. The paper flags that any adaptive-depth halting rule reading activation size will make bad calls in the small-norm regime — exactly when the dynamics are least trustworthy.
  • Why depth extrapolation saturates. The improvement per extra loop step decays at roughly the long-run growth rate. That is the mechanism behind the saturating-exponential test-time scaling law, and it lines up with Ouro peaking at its trained depth and degrading past it.
  • Mixture-of-Recursions. Per-channel carry has the same memory-versus-stability tension as diagonal SSMs (S4, Mamba) — only the axis differs (depth instead of sequence). One slow channel pins the whole margin: 31 channels at 0.5 plus one at 0.99 is as bad as all of them at 0.99.
