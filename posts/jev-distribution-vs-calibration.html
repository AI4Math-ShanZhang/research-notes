Jev vs the AR Model — Everyone Predicts a Distribution

Discussion note. Subject: Jev, TypeSafe AI's non-autoregressive "System One" model (Sept 2026) — announcement coverage, RLCD explainer, the skeptical read. No paper, no architecture disclosure, no model size.

✅ Conclusion

  • The pitch is wrong as stated. "LLMs predict tokens, we predict distributions" is false. An AR LLM trained with cross-entropy produces a full softmax distribution over the vocabulary at every step. It is readable from the logprobs without decoding anything.
  • The real difference is not the distribution, it is what the number means. Cross-entropy calibrates to text frequency, not to correctness — and RLHF then degrades calibration further (GPT-4 tech report: MMLU ECE ≈ 0.007 pre-RLHF → ≈ 0.074 post-RLHF).
  • Calibration ≠ accuracy. Two models can score identically and one number can still be useless. Accuracy reads one column; calibration reads the relationship between two.
  • Not overfitting. The post-RLHF model generalizes fine and more data makes confidence worse. This is Goodhart, not memorization.

📄 The claim, and why it does not hold

Jev returns a typed decision — a choice, a score, or a probability — instead of text. It runs prefill only and never enters the decode loop, which is where the speed claims come from (70–500 ms, 20–200× faster, output tokens free because there are none). The framing around it is that LLMs predict tokens whereas Jev predicts distributions.

That framing does not survive contact with how an AR model actually works:

flowchart LR A[context] --> B[h_final] B -->|W_unembed| C[logits] C -->|softmax| D["distribution over ~100k tokens"] D -->|sample ONE| E[token]

The distribution at step D is what cross-entropy training optimizes. It exists, it is complete, and several APIs will hand you the top-k logprobs directly. Stopping at D instead of E is not a new capability — it is declining to call sample(). That is the same move the confidence-routing paper in this category makes, and the same move zero-shot label scoring has made for years.

Candidate 2, with numbers

Ask a yes/no question, read the first-token distribution:

"Yes"   0.55      "No"    0.20
" Yes"  0.12      "Sure"  0.03
"yes"   0.04      "It"    0.02

Score only Yes vs No → 0.55 / (0.55 + 0.20) = 0.733.
Count every yes-variant → 0.71 / (0.71 + 0.20) = 0.780.

Two answers to the same question, depending on which tokens you decided to count. One concept's probability mass is smeared across several spellings, and more leaks into words that are neither label. A softmax over a vocabulary is genuinely not a distribution over your four options. Real problem — but plumbing, not principle.

Candidate 3 — the one that matters

Cross-entropy on tokens trains p(next token | context) — how often that token follows this context in text. It does not train p(my answer is correct). Different targets.

If enough of the corpus says "the capital of Australia is Sydney," the model puts high mass on Sydney. The distribution is perfectly calibrated to the corpus and wrong about the world. Cross-entropy did its job; the job was the wrong job.

The defensible version of Jev's pitch, then, is not "LLMs don't do distributions." It is: an LLM's distribution is over tokens, calibrated to text frequency, then damaged by RLHF — we train directly on decision correctness, so the number means what you think it means. That is a legitimate claim. The marketing version is not.

🖊 My Key Notes

What calibration actually means — the two-column view

The thing that kept not landing: calibration is not about being right. A model right only 30% of the time is perfectly calibrated if it says 30%. A forecaster who says "30% rain" and gets rain on 30% of those days is doing a perfect job.

The friend test. A friend says "I'll be there in 10 minutes." Friend A arrives in 10 — the number is trustworthy. Friend B always takes 30 — you learn to ignore the number and add 20. Friend B is not lying, their number just does not match reality. Now Friend B starts saying "5 minutes!" while still taking 30. More confident, worse.

The model emits two things per question: an answer (right or wrong) and a number. Accuracy reads the answer column only. Calibration reads the relationship between the two columns. Same ten questions, same answers, only the number column differs:

The test: cover the Right? column and look only at the number. Can you guess whether the answer is right? Honest model — yes (0.30 → go check it). Overconfident model — no, everything says ~0.9. RLHF did not destroy the answers. It destroyed the sorting power of the number.

Practical version: you have 10 answers and time to check 3. Honest model — check the three lowest (0.25, 0.30, 0.40) and you catch all three errors. Overconfident model — the three lowest are 0.90, 0.91, 0.91, which lands on Abidjan ✗, Bern ✓, Almaty ✗: two of three, one check wasted.

Why RLHF breaks it — and why perfect labels would not save you

The easy story is label noise: raters cannot verify who won the 1997 Nobel Prize in Physics, so they pick the confident-sounding answer. True, and it does push in the bad direction. But it is the shallow reason.

Assume perfect raters who know every fact and never err. One question, model's honest belief:

Paris 0.60   Lyon 0.25   Marseille 0.15

Truth is Paris, so the perfect rater picks the Paris answer — correct, no noise. RLHF nudges Paris up. Repeat thousands of times:

Paris 0.98   Lyon 0.01   Marseille 0.01

Still right on this question. But it was 60% sure and now claims 98%. Nothing was noisy; the pushing itself did it. Across 30 questions the model still gets 21 right — knowledge unchanged — while stated confidence climbs from an honest spread to a flat 0.9.

The deeper reason: a preference comparison answers "which answer is better?" It never says "your 80% should have been 60%." The reward signal contains no information about calibration at all, and you cannot learn something the reward never mentions. Perfect grading of the wrong quantity still teaches nothing about the right one.

This is not overfitting

The diagnostic: overfitting is cured by more data. Here more RLHF makes confidence worse. And the post-RLHF model generalizes — on fresh unseen questions humans really do prefer its answers. That is successful learning of a transferable skill; it is just a skill nobody meant to trade calibration for. Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure.

Worth separating from a real overfitting problem that does live inside RLHF — reward-model over-optimization, where RM score keeps climbing while true human preference falls, which is why the KL penalty gets capped. That one is genuine overfitting, and fixing it completely would still leave calibration broken.

How you would actually fix it

Stop asking humans, score the number against the outcome. Squared error, outcome 1 for right and 0 for wrong:

SaidOutcomeLoss
0.80right(0.8−1)² = 0.04
0.80wrong(0.8−0)² = 0.64
0.99right0.0001 — big prize
0.99wrong0.98 — big punishment

The property that makes this work: suppose the model is truly right 70% of the time on some class of question. What should it report?

StrategyAverage loss
always say 0.95 (bluff high)0.7×0.05² + 0.3×0.95² = 0.2725
always say 0.50 (play safe)0.7×0.5² + 0.3×0.5² = 0.2500
always say 0.70 (the truth)0.7×0.3² + 0.3×0.7² = 0.2100 ← lowest

Telling the truth wins. Bluffing high gets crushed by the 30% of times you are wrong; playing safe wastes the 70% you are right. There is no way to game it — the only route to lower loss is reporting your real belief. That is what a proper scoring rule means, and it is the exact opposite of RLHF, where bluffing pays.

The honest part

  • RLCD is undisclosed. No paper, no architecture, no model size. The scoring-rule mechanism above is the standard way calibration training works and the only way their claim could hold — it is not a quote from them. Treat the calibration claim as unverified.
  • The trick itself is old. Score each label, normalize, read a distribution over options invented seconds ago. That is zero-shot classification, and you can do it today with any open model by grabbing the step-1 logits and not decoding. Hence "why you already have one."
  • Benchmarks are self-reported, on workflows TypeSafe's own team wrote, with reference answers averaged from competing models.
  • "Cannot hallucinate" means cannot emit an invalid schema. It can still classify wrong — the same limit as a confidence probe, which catches "I don't know" but not "I'm sure and I'm wrong."
Where it connects: the confidence-routing paper runs the identical prefill-only trick, but asks a meta-level question ("will I hallucinate?") rather than an object-level one ("is this fraudulent?"). It also thresholds at 0.75 / 0.55 / 0.35 while never measuring calibration once — which, given everything above, is the one thing those thresholds depend on.
