d-OPSD — Learning from the Self-future

Paper: Learning from the Self-future: On-policy Self-distillation for dLLMs — Yifu Luo, Zeyu Chen, Haoyu Wang, Xinhao Hu, Yuxuan Zhang, Zhizhou Sha, Shiwei Liu, arXiv:2606.18195. Code: xingzhejun/d-OPSD.

📄 Paper Summary

On-policy self-distillation gives a teacher extra information the student cannot see, and trains the student to match the teacher on states the student itself visited. Existing versions are built for autoregressive models: the privileged information arrives as a left-to-right prefix, and the supervision is a token-level divergence. A diffusion LM does not generate left to right, so that shape does not fit. d-OPSD changes both halves. The teacher is the same model shown hint tokens taken from the student's own completed answer — the self-future — pasted back into an earlier, still-masked state; and the supervision is applied per denoising step instead of per token. On four reasoning benchmarks it beats RLVR and SFT while using roughly 10% of RLVR's optimization steps.

Q1 — What are OPD and OPSD?

On-policy distillation (OPD) trains on trajectories generated by the current student. A teacher evaluates the states the student visited and supplies probability distributions for the student to match.

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) builds the teacher from the same underlying model, giving it extra information the student cannot see.

QuestionOPDOPSD
Who generates the trajectory?StudentStudent
Who provides supervision?Usually a separate, stronger modelA teacher derived from the same model
What gives the teacher an advantage?Stronger capabilitiesPrivileged context, such as a reference solution

“Self” does not mean identical predictions: different contexts produce different distributions. In this paper's default setup the teacher is frozen at the initial policy while the student keeps updating, so their parameters drift apart too.

Background: OPD paper, OPSD paper.

Q2 — Why train on student generations if they can be wrong?

Keep two things apart: where the training states come from and what the learning targets are.

  • The student generates the states.
  • The teacher supplies the target distributions.

If the student writes 6 × 7 = 43, the teacher can still put its mass on 42 at the context 6 × 7 =. The sampled 43 is not automatically treated as a correct label.

The paper also filters. By default it samples until a correct response appears, up to eight attempts, and computes the loss only on accepted generations; Sudoku uses a score threshold instead. So the default method still leans on an external correctness signal even though the response text is student-generated. And a correct final answer does not by itself verify every reasoning step along the way.

Q3 — What does Figure 2 show? Why does the teacher get “a bus”?

The right-hand example is:

Student state y_t: He is at the [M] [M] [M] get [M] [M] [M]
Completed y_0:     He is at the bus stop to  get on   a   bus
Teacher input:     He is at the [M] [M] [M] get [M]  a   bus

[M] is a masked token. The teacher has the same number of sequence positions, just two fewer masks.

The student finishes its rollout first. Training then goes back to a saved intermediate state and reveals selected tokens from the completed response at their original positions — here, a bus.

The teacher uses that extra context to produce its distributions. It does not have to generate a second complete response to supply the distillation targets.

Q4 — How are the hint tokens and the top-k positions chosen?

These are two separate selections:

SelectionRulePurpose
Hint positions, such as a bus Randomly pick currently masked positions; reveal their values from the completed response Give the teacher extra context
Positions supervised by the loss Take the top-k confident positions, using teacher confidence by default Decide where to compare vocabulary distributions

The default hint ratio is 25% of currently masked positions, so it is not always exactly two tokens. Hints do not have to be a contiguous phrase and do not have to sit at the end.

The original student rollout picks decoding positions by the student's own confidence. The default loss selection uses the teacher's confidence. Note that top-k here means positions in the response, not the top k vocabulary words.

The random hints can land on the same positions as the student's top-k. Distillation does not need to avoid that. In Figure 2 the a bus hints and the yellow loss positions happen to be different.

Q5 — Is the teacher exactly one step ahead?

It is ahead in information, but its input is not necessarily the student's actual next state.

Current student:     He is at the [M] [M] [M] get [M] [M] [M]
Possible next state: He is at the bus stop [M] get [M] [M] [M]
Constructed teacher: He is at the [M] [M] [M] get [M]  a   bus

Both enriched states have two extra visible tokens, but at different positions. The constructed teacher state might never occur anywhere in the original rollout.

If the actual next step happens to reveal exactly the selected hints, the two states coincide. The method does not require that.

The paper counts denoising downward: the next state after y_t is y_(t−1).

Q6 — Does every y_t need its own completed y_0?

No. One rollout produces one trajectory and one completed response:

y_T → y_(T−1) → … → y_1 → y_0

Each saved state gets its own constructed teacher input, with hints drawn from that same y_0. The hint positions can differ from step to step. A new rollout brings its own trajectory and its own completed response.

Q7 — Could we use a fresh continuation, or a teacher exactly one step ahead?

Yes — two alternatives worth naming.

A. Complete a fresh continuation from every saved state

From each y_t, sample a new complete response and take the hints from it. That would give more varied hints, but costs far more generation: with T steps, one fresh completion from every state is roughly T(T+1)/2 denoising steps against T for the shared rollout, before retries and teacher evaluation.

It still does not guarantee a true one-step-ahead teacher state.

B. Use the actual next state as teacher context

Student context: y_t
Teacher context: y_(t−1)

This really does give the teacher one extra denoising step of context. It is a possible experiment, not the paper's default construction. Whether it helps depends on whether those extra predictions are good supervision — wrong reveals can mislead the teacher. Loss-position selection and correctness filtering would both need to be respecified.

Q8 — How does this differ from standard dLLM training?

Standard masked-diffusion trainingd-OPSD
DataClean dataset responseStudent-generated trajectory
StatesUsually one randomly corrupted state per example at a sampled noise level Saved states across a complete generation trajectory
TargetOriginal tokens at masked positions Teacher vocabulary distributions at selected positions
 
Standard training:
Clean response → random masking → model prediction → loss

d-OPSD:
Student rollout → save states and completed response
                → construct teacher context for each state
                → average teacher–student losses → update student

The trajectory has T denoising steps, but that does not mean T optimizer updates. The paper averages losses across the trajectory and batches the saved states for training. Standard dLLMs still use iterative denoising at inference.
