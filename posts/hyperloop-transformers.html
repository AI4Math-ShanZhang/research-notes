Hyperloop Transformers

Paper: Hyperloop Transformers — Abbas Zeitoun, Lucas Torroba-Hennigen & Yoon Kim (MIT), arXiv:2604.21254. July 2026 (v3).

✅ Three things to remember

  1. A hyper-connection is just a residual connection with more wires. Instead of one stream and a fixed "+1" skip, you carry n streams and the skip becomes a small learned matrix. The attention and MLP still see one normal-width vector, so compute barely moves.
  2. Apply them once per loop, not once per layer. That is the whole trick. 3 loops means 3 hyper-connections. About 200K extra parameters, and 95% of baseline training speed — where the every-layer version (mHC) runs at 65%.
  3. A looped model repeats itself, and that is the problem being fixed. In a plain looped model the same layer gives a 0.92-similar output in every loop. Hyperloop drops that to 0.87, and the perplexity drops with it.

Headline: 579.7M parameters, perplexity 9.65 — against a 990.5M Transformer at 10.19. Half the parameters, better number. This survives INT4 quantization (9.81 vs 10.27).

📄 Part 1 — The notation

Everything in this paper is one equation. Here it is up front, so you know where we are going:

y_new = H_res · y_old + H_post · F( H_pre · y_old )

Eight symbols. Here they are.

T — how many tokens in the input.
Say 100. Nothing in this paper depends on it.
C — the width of one residual stream.
The normal model dimension. 1024 for the small models in the paper, 2048 for the big ones.
n — how many parallel streams. The expansion factor.
This is the new thing. The paper uses n = 4. A normal transformer is n = 1.
x — an ordinary residual vector for one token.
C numbers. 1024 of them.
y — the expanded residual for one token.
n × C. A 4 × 1024 matrix. Four separate 1024-vectors stacked up. This is what goes around the loop instead of x.
F — the actual work.
One attention layer, or one MLP layer, or the whole middle block. It takes C numbers in and gives C numbers out. It never sees the expanded thing. Remember this — it is why the method is cheap.
H_pre — the read weights. Shape 1 × n.
Blends the 4 streams down into 1 stream, so F has something normal-sized to chew on.
H_post — the write weights. Shape n × 1.
Takes the single output of F and decides how much of it goes into each of the 4 streams.
H_res — the skip weights. Shape n × n.
The old residual "+1", generalized. Lets the streams mix into each other without going through F at all.
Set n = 1 and H_pre = H_post = H_res = 1 and the big equation collapses to x_new = x_old + F(x_old). A hyper-connection is a generalized skip connection. That is the one-line definition.

Two more, for the loop side

l — which loop iteration you are on.
The paper uses 3 loops, so l is 0, 1, 2.
e_l — the loop position embedding. C numbers.
A learned vector added after the middle block, different for each loop. It tells the shared block "you are on pass number 2 now."

🖊 Part 2 — The story, one step at a time

Step 1. Cut the transformer into three pieces

This part is not new — it is the "middle cycle" recipe from Bae et al. 2025. Take a transformer and split it into begin, middle, end. Loop only the middle.

Parameter budget in this paper: 25% begin, 50% middle, 25% end. Loop the middle 3 times.

So a model written 2L → 4L (×3) → 2L has 2 begin layers, 4 middle layers run three times, and 2 end layers. You store 8 layers. You execute 16.

stored : 2 + 4 + 2 = 8 layers → 135M params executed : 2 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 2 = 16 layers → same cost as a 238M Transformer
Read this carefully: looping saves memory, not compute. You still run 16 layers of matmuls. You just only keep 8 layers of weights around. That is the whole point of the paper — it targets phones and edge devices, where RAM is the wall, not FLOPs.

Problem: plain looped transformers are worse than a depth-matched transformer on perplexity. Table 1 shows it — 14.85 for looped vs 14.65 for the 238M transformer. That gap is what Hyperloop closes.

Step 2. Why looping loses: the model repeats itself

The middle block has one set of weights. You run it three times. What stops it from doing the same thing all three times?

Nothing. And it largely does. The paper measures this directly (Table 8) — take the output of middle layer 1 in loop 0, and the output of middle layer 1 in loop 1, and compare them:

model sizeplain LoopedHyperloop
136M0.7430.738
579M0.9150.872
991M0.9230.871

0.92 similarity means loop 1 is almost a copy of loop 0. You paid for 3 passes and got maybe 1.5 passes of new work.

The fix the paper proposes: give the loop a place to store side information that changes between passes. That place is the extra parallel streams.

Step 3. What a hyper-connection actually is

Start from the normal residual connection:

x_new = x_old + F(x_old)

One stream. The weight on the skip path is hard-coded to 1. Hyper-connections (Zhu et al. 2025a, then Xie et al. 2026) change three things at once:

  1. One stream becomes n streams. Carry 4 vectors of 1024 instead of 1 vector of 1024.
  2. The fixed "+1" becomes a learned n × n matrix. Streams can now pour into each other.
  3. Reading and writing become learned too. A weighted blend goes in; a weighted spread comes out.

4 streams × 1024"]
Why this is nearly free: look at the box in the middle. F takes 1024 in and gives 1024 out, exactly as before. Attention and the MLP are where all the FLOPs live, and they did not change. You only added three small matrices on the outside.

Step 4. One pass, with real numbers

Use n = 2 streams and C = 3, so it fits on the page.

Start. Two streams for one token:

stream A = [1, 0, 2] stream B = [0, 3, 1]

Read. H_pre = [0.8, 0.2]. Blend into one vector:

0.8·A + 0.2·B = [0.8, 0.6, 1.8] ← this is what attention/MLP receives

Work. Say F returns:

u = [0.1, -0.5, 0.4]

Write. H_post = [1.5, 0.5]. Stream A gets 1.5 of it, stream B gets 0.5 of it:

into A : [0.15, -0.75, 0.6] into B : [0.05, -0.25, 0.2]

Mix. H_res = [[0.9, 0.1], [0.2, 0.8]]:

A base = 0.9·A + 0.1·B = [0.9, 0.3, 1.9] B base = 0.2·A + 0.8·B = [0.2, 2.4, 1.2]

Add.

A_new = [1.05, -0.45, 2.5] B_new = [0.25, 2.15, 1.4]

Done. One hyper-connected step. Notice stream B kept its big 2.15 in the middle slot while stream A went negative there — the two streams are now carrying different things. That is the capacity being bought.

Step 5. The H matrices are computed, not stored

This is the part people miss. H_pre, H_post, H_res are not weights. They are outputs, recomputed for every single token from that token's current state. The weights are the things that produce them.

z = RMSNorm(flatten(y)) ← 4×1024 squashed to 4096 H_pre = sigmoid( α_pre · (W_pre · z) ) + b_pre H_post = 2 · sigmoid( α_post · (W_post · z) ) + b_post H_res = sinkhorn( α_res · reshape(W_res · z) + b_res )

Sizes, for n = 4, C = 1024:

thingsizejob
W_pre4 × 4096turn the state into 4 raw scores
α_pre1 numbertemperature — how sharply to react
b_pre4 numbersshift the result
W_res16 × 409616 numbers, reshaped into a 4 × 4 grid

The α is a learned scalar — one single trainable number. It does not change the scores, it changes how sharply the sigmoid reacts to them. Say the raw scores are [2.0, -3.0]:

αα × scoresafter sigmoidbehaviour
0.1[0.2, -0.3][0.550, 0.426]both near 0.5 — basically a fixed connection
1.0[2.0, -3.0][0.881, 0.047]genuinely reacts to the input
10[20, -30][1.000, 0.000]saturated — a hard on/off switch

So α is a learned knob for how input-dependent this connection should be. Start it small and the model begins life behaving like an ordinary residual connection, which is known to train well; it can turn the knob up later if that helps.

Why H_post has the extra 2. With α small, sigmoid gives 0.5, and 2 × 0.5 = 1.0. So the default write weight is exactly 1 — a plain residual connection. The 2 exists to put "do nothing special" at the right place.

What sinkhorn does. Take the 4 × 4 grid and normalize rows, then columns, then rows again, ~20 times. You end up with every row summing to 1 and every column summing to 1 ("doubly stochastic"). That means the mixing is a soft shuffle — nothing is created, nothing is destroyed, so the residual cannot quietly explode or die over depth. The constraint is what "manifold-constrained" (mHC) refers to.

Step 6. What Hyperloop changes — three edits

Everything up to here is background (§2.2). Here is the actual paper (§3). Three edits to mHC:

Edit 1 — throw away sinkhorn, use a diagonal.

H_res = diag( sigmoid( α_res · (W_res · z) + b_res ) )

Diagonal means no mixing between streams at all. Each stream just gets its own gate between 0 and 1. W_res shrinks from 16 × 4096 to 4 × 4096. And it is not a compromise — it wins:

H_res parameterizationPPL
Identity (no gate at all)14.61
Sinkhorn (mHC's version)14.59
Diagonal (this paper)14.40

Edit 2 — add the loop position embedding e_l. A learned C-vector added after the middle block, different for each loop. The recurrence becomes:

y^(l+1) = H_res · y^(l) + H_post · ( F( H_pre · y^(l) ) + e_l )

The paper's framing: read this as a depth-wise RNN whose hidden state is a matrix. The initial Y^(0) is the input, fed in at every time step, and e_l is the step counter.

Edit 3 — apply hyper-connections only at the loop boundary. Not after every attention and MLP. 3 loops, 3 hyper-connections, that is all. Inside the middle block the layers keep their own ordinary skip connections and know nothing about the 4 streams.

This one sounds like a pure cost saving, so you would expect it to hurt quality. It does not:

hyper-connectionswherePPL
12every layer14.45
6every 2 layers14.50
4every 3 layers14.50
3every loop (this paper)14.40
2every 6 layers14.50
1every 12 layers14.63
The cheapest setting is also the best one, and it beats the setting with 4× the parameters and compute. The paper's own words: "perhaps unintuitive." Placement matters more than quantity — being aligned with the loop boundary beats being frequent.

Step 7. The whole forward pass

X_begin = begin_block(tokens)          # T × 1024
Y = stack([X_begin] * 4)               # duplicate → T × 4 × 1024

for l in range(3):                     # 3 loops
    z      = rmsnorm(flatten(Y))       # T × 4096
    H_pre  =     sigmoid(a_pre[l]  * z @ W_pre[l].T)  + b_pre[l]    # T × 4
    H_post = 2 * sigmoid(a_post[l] * z @ W_post[l].T) + b_post[l]   # T × 4
    H_res  =     sigmoid(a_res[l]  * z @ W_res[l].T   + b_res[l])   # T × 4 (diagonal)

    inp = einsum('tn,tnc->tc', H_pre, Y)          # blend 4 streams → 1
    out = middle_block(inp) + e[l]                # 4 shared layers + loop embedding
    Y   = H_res[...,None] * Y + H_post[...,None] * out[:,None,:]

X_end = Y.mean(dim=1)                  # average the 4 streams → T × 1024
logits = lm_head(end_block(X_end))

Two bookends worth naming, because they are the simplest possible choice in both cases: duplicate to get from 1 stream to 4 at the start, and average to get from 4 back to 1 at the end. No learned projection at either end.

Parameter cost. Per loop: three W matrices at about 16K each, plus e_l at 1024, plus 3 α scalars and a handful of biases. Roughly 50K per loop, 150K for three loops. That matches the measured 135.7M − 135.5M = 200K. The α's are 9 numbers out of 200,000.

Three things to read out of it:

  • Half the parameters, better perplexity. 579.7M beats 990.5M, 9.65 vs 10.19.
  • Speed is the quiet win. Hyperloop runs at 95–99% of baseline throughput. mHC runs at 65%. Same idea, but applied 12 times instead of 3, in plain PyTorch.
  • Quantization holds. Plain Looped degrades badly under INT4 (14.85 → 15.18, and 8.68 → 8.97). Hyperloop degrades like a normal transformer (8.49 → 8.59). The worry was that looped layers, having seen more inputs, would be harder to quantize. Hyper-connections absorb some of that.

Also worth knowing (Table 3): a 3L → 2L (×6) → 3L Hyperloop with 477M parameters scores 9.86, still under the 990.5M Transformer's 10.19. And under heavy overtraining (Table 2, 100B tokens = 20× Chinchilla) plain Looped falls apart (12.56 vs 12.15) while Hyperloop stays level (12.19).

Step 9. Is this just LoRA on a looped model?

Fair question — the hyper-connection weights differ per loop, so the looped block does behave slightly differently each pass. LoRA per loop step (Bae et al.'s relaxed recursive transformers) does the same thing more directly. The paper ran it:

LoRA rank per loopparamsPPL
0 (plain looped)135.5M14.85
4136.0M14.85
8137.0M14.81
16139.0M14.80
32143.0M14.77
Hyperloop136.0M14.40

LoRA rank 32 spends 7.5M extra parameters to gain 0.08. Hyperloop spends 0.5M to gain 0.45. So "vary the weights across loops" is not the mechanism — if it were, LoRA would have caught up. Something about the extra streams is doing the work.

Step 10. Figure 3 — the evidence for why it works

Nine heatmaps. Rows are model sizes (135M / 580M / 1B), columns are architectures (Transformer / Looped / Hyperloop).

How each one was made:

  1. Run 50M held-out FineWeb-Edu tokens through the trained model.
  2. At every effective layer — unrolled, so middle-layer-2-in-loop-0 and middle-layer-2-in-loop-1 are two separate points — record the residual vector. For Hyperloop they record the inner stream, the C-dim thing that actually enters attention, i.e. H_pre · y.
  3. For every pair of layers (i, j), compute cosine similarity, averaged over tokens.
  4. Draw the grid. Row = layer i, column = layer j. Blue = near 1, red = near 0. The main diagonal is always dark blue, because a layer is identical to itself.

The axis labels. On the looped panels you see 2(0), 4(0), 2(1), 4(1)… That reads "middle layer 2, during loop 0". The dashed lines mark where one loop ends and the next starts. e at the end is the end block.

What you see:

  • Transformer (left): a smooth gradient. Nearby layers similar, far layers less so. The last row and column turn red — the final layer swings hard to line up with the vocabulary. Ordinary.
  • Looped (middle): square blocks between the dashed lines, and — the thing to actually look at — bright blue stripes running parallel to the main diagonal, one loop-length away from it. That stripe is the repetition. Same layer, different loop, nearly the same output.
  • Hyperloop (right): that parallel stripe is visibly lighter. Same layer, different loop, genuinely different output.
Table 8 is that stripe, as a number. 0.915 → 0.872 at 579M, 0.923 → 0.871 at 991M. Lower means each loop pass does more new work, which is the whole claim.
The rereading analogy. A looped model is a student reading the same page three times. If you take no notes, the third reading is the first reading again — 0.92 similarity. The four parallel streams are a margin to scribble in: what you already worked out, what is still confusing. Now the second read starts from a different place, so it gets somewhere different. And you only need to update the margin between readings, not after every sentence — which is exactly why one hyper-connection per loop beats twelve per layer.

Step 11. Figure 4 — the logit lens, and a free hint

Different analysis, same 50M tokens. Take the outer residual stream at each effective layer, push it straight through the language-model head, and look at what comes out. Three curves: cross-entropy, entropy, and greedy-decoding accuracy.

Result: both looped models line up with the vocabulary much earlier than a plain transformer, with alignment peaking at the end of each loop. Hyperloop lines up most. The paper reads this as hyper-connections adding a regularizing pull toward vocabulary space.

The practical hint: if the answer is already readable halfway through, you can stop early. The paper flags early-exit inference as the obvious follow-up — that would turn a memory-efficiency result into a compute-efficiency one. Not attempted here.

⚠ How much to trust it

  • Scale — the paper's own stated limitation. Largest model is 990M looped / 2B depth-matched, on academic compute. Whether "half the parameters" survives at frontier scale is untested.
  • "50% fewer parameters" is not "50% cheaper." Hyperloop executes the same unrolled depth as the baseline it beats, so FLOPs per token are roughly matched. The saving is memory, full stop. The paper is upfront about this — it is the premise, not a hidden claim — but the abstract reads better than the compute story.
  • Figure 3 and Table 8 are correlational. The paper says "suggestive evidence" and "supporting our hypothesis," never that lower similarity causes lower perplexity. It could be a side effect of a model that is simply fitting better.
  • The small model shows nothing. 0.743 vs 0.738 at 136M is noise. The similarity story only appears at 579M and up — on 2 data points.
  • The mHC speed comparison is not apples-to-apples, and the paper says so. mHC's 514K tok/s is a naive PyTorch implementation; Xie et al. report only 6.7% overhead with a custom kernel, which is not public. The honest version of the claim is "no sophisticated systems engineering required," not "mHC is slow."
  • More streams barely helps. Table 4: n=2 → 14.43, n=4 → 14.40, n=10 → 14.35. Almost flat, for 2.5× the hyper-connection parameters. So n is not a scaling axis, and the reason the method works probably is not "more capacity in the residual."
  • Two shape inconsistencies in the writing. Page 3 states all three H matrices are n × n, which does not typecheck against §2.2's own equation (they must be 1×n, n×1, n×n for the dimensions to work). And Table 3's first Hyperloop row reads 3L → 4L (×3) → 2L where Table 1 has → 3L. Both look like typos, neither affects the results.

Verdict: the engineering result is solid and cheap to reproduce — three edits, 200K parameters, and the ablations honestly report that the cheapest variant won. The explanation (Figure 3) is the weakest part and is presented as such.

🔗 Hooks to the rest of this category

  • Ouro. Ouro found looping adds knowledge manipulation but not knowledge capacity, and that performance peaks at the trained depth (4) and falls apart by 8. Figure 2 here is the matching curve from the other side — Hyperloop keeps improving from 2 to 6 loops but with clear diminishing returns. Both papers are describing the same ceiling. Ouro's early-exit gate is also the natural partner for the logit-lens finding in Step 11.
  • LayerNorm as implicit gain control. That paper's loop is h_next = A·h + f(h,e) with A a per-channel carry in (0,1). Hyperloop's H_res = diag(sigmoid(...)) is exactly that — a per-stream gate in (0,1) — except input-dependent instead of static. So the stability analysis should carry over, including the warning that the dangerous direction is activations shrinking, not growing.
  • Mixture-of-Recursions. MoR varies depth per token. Hyperloop varies the connection per token while depth stays fixed. They are orthogonal and nobody has combined them.
  • The boosted-loop framing. Hyperloop is loop-specific {W, b, α, e} — a step-conditioned adapter by another name. Table 7 is the important data point: LoRA-per-loop gets you 0.08 and Hyperloop gets 0.45, which says the gain is not coming from letting weights vary across steps. Any claim that a per-step adapter is the mechanism has to explain that gap.
